# Research evidence: how strong each effect really is

Read this before leaning on a psychology effect to justify a design. Each principle below gives the strength of the evidence, the rule that the evidence actually supports, and the claims from the source videos that go further than the research does. The full entries, with citations, URLs and numbers, are in `sources/articles/cited-research.md`.

Strength scale:

- **Strong**: large, replicated, meta-analysed, holds across settings.
- **Mixed**: real, but conditional, small, or contested in the literature.
- **Weak**: one small study, unreplicated, or no traceable source.

## At a glance

| Principle | Strength | Use it as |
|---|---|---|
| Defaults | Strong | Pre-select the option most people would pick anyway |
| Choice overload | Mixed | Structure hard choices for unsure users; do not cap every list at six |
| Goal gradient | Strong in reward programmes | Show the remaining distance; plan the slump after a reward |
| Endowed progress | Mixed (one field study, plus a related replication) | Count real completed steps, give the reason |
| IKEA effect | Mixed to strong, needs completion | Let users finish something small before the ask |
| Reciprocity | Mixed (real, size varies) | Give real value first, personal and unexpected |
| Loss aversion | Strong for money gambles, mixed as a messaging rule | Name real stakes; never invent a loss |
| Contrast and anchoring | Strong for anchoring and relative price; weak for decoy plans | Put a price after its context; skip fake decoys |
| Fluency and aesthetics | Strong for perception, mixed for "premium" | Make it easy to see; prove quality with real evidence |
| Consistency | Strong as a standard, weak as the cited data | Same nav, same place, same order, every screen |
| Service recovery | Mixed (satisfaction yes, loyalty no) | Recover fast and well; never plan on failure |

## Defaults

**Evidence: strong.** A meta-analysis of 58 datasets (73,675 people) found defaults raise uptake of the pre-selected option by d = 0.68, about 27 percentage points on average (Jachimowicz et al. 2019). In Microsoft Word, fewer than 5% of users changed any setting (Spool 2011). Defaults work best when they read as a recommendation or as "what you already have". The effect varies hugely, and some defaults backfire.

**Honest rule:** pre-select the choice most users would make if asked, and make it easy to change. In Johnson and Goldstein's organ donor survey, the forced choice result (79% yes) was close to the opt-out result (82%), which is what a good default looks like. A default that serves the business against the user (pre-ticked add-ons, opt-in to marketing) is a dark pattern, and once users notice it the "recommendation" signal turns into distrust.

**Overstated in the videos:**

- "70 to 90% of users in most products never change the default values" (2TlIg3VokY8). No source exists for this number. The closest real figure is "more than 95% never touched Word's settings", from one informal study of one app.
- The organ donation story is usually told as defaults "saving lives". Registered consent differs by about 60 points between opt-in and opt-out countries, but actual donations differ by about 16%, and a 2019 comparison found no significant difference in deceased donors. Defaults change what is recorded far more than what happens.

## Choice overload

**Evidence: mixed.** The jam study (Iyengar and Lepper 2000) is real: of people who stopped at the booth, 30% bought after seeing 6 jams versus 3% after 24. But the 24 jam display drew more people to stop (60% versus 40%), it ran on two Saturdays in one store, and direct replications in Germany found nothing. Scheibehenne et al. (2010) pooled 50 experiments and found an average effect of almost zero (D = 0.02). Chernev et al. (2015) pooled 99 observations and found overload is real when four conditions hold: options are complex or hard to compare, the task is difficult or rushed, the user has no clear preference, and the user wants to minimise effort. People with expertise or a clear preference do better with more options.

**Honest rule:** when users are new, unsure or in a hurry and the options are hard to compare, cut the visible set, group it, and mark one recommended choice. When users know what they want (search, catalogues, power tools), give them the full set with search, filters and sorting instead of hiding items.

**Overstated in the videos:**

- "24 flavours 3% bought, 6 flavours 30% bought", stated as a general law (2TlIg3VokY8). True of one booth; not replicated; the meta-analytic average is about zero.
- "6 options converted 10x better than 24", turned into "show at most 4 to 6" (rule 4 in the 2TlIg3VokY8 note). The research supports reducing hard choices for unsure users, not a fixed cap.
- "Columbia University jam study": the store was Draeger's in Menlo Park, and the paper is Columbia and Stanford. A detail, but it shows the story is retold, not read.

## Goal gradient

**Evidence: strong within reward programmes, consistent with decades of animal work.** Members of a café coffee card bought about 20% more often as they neared the free coffee; music raters visited more and rated more near the reward; they slowed again after earning it (Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng 2006).

**Honest rule:** show the remaining distance in small, concrete units ("2 more lessons"), make the last steps feel short, and start the next goal right after a reward, because effort resets once a reward is earned.

**Overstated in the videos:** the car wash card study is presented under the "goal gradient" heading as a Columbia study (2TlIg3VokY8). It is the endowed progress study by Nunes and Drèze (USC and Wharton). The Columbia study is the coffee card one.

## Endowed progress

**Evidence: mixed.** One field study: car wash cards needing 8 stamps (0 of 8) versus 10 stamps with 2 pre-stamped. Completion was 34% versus 19%, and the endowed group returned 2.9 days sooner (Nunes and Drèze 2006, 300 cards, one site). The coffee card experiment in Kivetz et al. (12 stamps with 2 bonus versus 10) found completion about 20% faster, a related replication. The head start works better when a reason is given for it, and even an arbitrary reason works.

**Honest rule:** never show 0% when the user has truly done something. Count the real steps already completed (account created, email confirmed) and say why they count. Do not invent progress that does not exist, and do not inflate a percentage the user can check against reality.

**Overstated in the videos:**

- "Progress, even a fake one, creates real momentum" (2TlIg3VokY8 recap). The studies used real stamps with a stated offer, in loyalty cards, at modest effect sizes. Fabricated progress in a product the user can inspect risks the trust the effect depends on.
- "Group B completed at nearly double the rate". Accurate (34% versus 19%, 1.8 times), but from a single site with a few hundred cards.

## IKEA effect and endowment

**Evidence: mixed to strong.** People who assembled an IKEA box bid $0.78 versus $0.48 for a pre-built one; novice origami makers valued their own work about five times higher than others did, close to what others paid for experts' work (Norton, Mochon and Ariely 2012). A conceptual replication supported it (Sarstedt et al. 2017). Key condition: the effect disappeared when the creation was destroyed or left unfinished. The endowment effect (owners value a mug above what buyers will pay, Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler 1990) is well known, though careful elicitation shrinks it.

**Honest rule:** before asking for an account, let the user make one small thing and finish it (a profile card, a first lesson, a first project), and show it as theirs. Keep the task short enough to finish; an abandoned setup creates no attachment and may create frustration.

**Overstated in the videos:**

- "Label the button 'Continue' rather than 'Sign up', so leaving feels like abandoning their work" (2TlIg3VokY8). That button wording was never tested; it is the author's idea. Hiding that a step creates an account is a transparency problem.
- The studies used cents-level values and simple objects with students. The effect is real; the size in a product is unknown.

## Reciprocity

**Evidence: mixed (real, size varies).** A small chocolate with the restaurant bill raised tips, and the amount and manner of giving mattered (Strohmetz et al. 2002). Cialdini's own page reports +3% for one mint, +14% for two, +23% when the waiter came back with a personal extra mint. A related study found no effect of its reciprocity manipulation. In-store sampling lifts sales for weeks (Dotson et al., BYU), with no reliable headline percentage.

**Honest rule:** deliver real value before any ask (show the result, then offer to save it), and make the gift feel personal. Frame sign up as keeping what the user already has. This is good practice regardless of how big the reciprocity effect is, because a wall before value simply loses people.

**Overstated in the videos:**

- "Robert Cialdini ranked reciprocity as the single most powerful driver of behaviour" (2TlIg3VokY8). Cialdini's own material lists his principles side by side with no ranking.
- "Free samples in grocery stores increase purchases by up to 2,000%". No primary source exists. It is a recycled outlier anecdote; the sampling firm behind Costco's demos is quoted with 71% for beer as a typical figure.

## Loss aversion and loss framing

**Evidence: strong for decisions about money; mixed as a rule for wording.** A meta-analysis of 607 estimates found losses weigh about 1.8 to 2.1 times as much as equal gains (median 1.69) in gambles and trades (Brown et al. 2024). Gal and Rucker (2018) argue the general claim is overstated and context dependent. For messages, which is what a modal headline is, meta-analyses find loss-framed and gain-framed appeals about equally persuasive (O'Keefe and Jensen 2007, 93 studies, r = .03 in favour of gain framing; 2009, r = -.04 in favour of loss framing for screening only).

**Honest rule:** when the user really stands to lose something (files, a streak, an unsaved draft, a deadline that truly exists), say so concretely: name the items, the amount and the date. That is useful information, not manipulation. Never invent a loss, a countdown or a deletion threat to drive an upgrade; fake urgency is a deceptive pattern, and in storage it erodes trust in the one thing the product is for.

**Overstated in the videos:**

- "Kahneman showed losses are about twice as powerful as gains", applied as "flip the framing on every screen that asks users to act" (2TlIg3VokY8). The factor of about 2 is from money gambles (Tversky and Kahneman 1992, median 2.25, a joint result; Tversky died before the 2002 prize). As a copywriting rule, loss framing does not reliably beat gain framing.
- The "Your Files Will Be Deleted" upgrade modal with "I'll Risk It" as the dismiss link. Fine only if the deletion is real policy. "I'll Risk It" is confirmshaming; keep the dismiss neutral ("Not now").

## Contrast, anchoring and decoys

**Evidence: strong for anchoring and relative price, weak for decoys.** Anchoring is one of the most replicated effects in judgement research (Tversky and Kahneman 1974). Relative thinking is classic: people go out of their way to save $5 on a $15 item, but not on a $125 item (Tversky and Kahneman 1981). Estate agents' valuations moved with the list price they were shown (Northcraft and Neale 1987). The decoy (attraction) effect, adding a dominated option to push the target, mostly appears with numeric attributes on paper. With real naturalistic choices built to its own authors' criteria it measured "precisely zero" (Trendl, Stewart and Mullett 2021).

**Honest rule:** show an add-on price next to the thing it protects and in proportion ("2.6% of your order"), and decide which number the user sees first on a pricing page. Make sure every plan on a pricing page is one you would honestly sell; do not add a junk plan to make another look good.

**Overstated in the videos:** "The $90 Wagyu makes the $40 salmon look reasonable" and "agents show an overpriced house first" (2TlIg3VokY8) are told as sure tricks. Anchoring supports the general idea; the deliberate decoy version is the weakest part of this literature.

## Fluency, aesthetics and premium perception

**Evidence: strong that fluency raises liking; mixed that looks create "premium".** Things that are easier to perceive (clear contrast, symmetry, familiar form, legible type) are judged more beautiful, a theory well supported in the review by Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman (2004); the boost fades when people attribute the ease to something irrelevant. Aesthetic packages were picked more often (73% versus 54%), even over a known brand at a higher price (51% versus 42%), and activated reward areas in the brain (Reimann et al. 2010); these were hypothetical yes or no choices on pictures, with 17 people in the scanner. In Pombo and Velasco (2021), a quality claim on the label drove premiumness strongly, while symmetry raised it only some of the time, and curvature raised liking but not premiumness.

**Honest rule:** make every screen effortless to perceive (contrast, alignment, one type family, predictable patterns), because ease reads as quality. To be seen as premium, show evidence of real quality: complete states, accurate feedback, care in the details. Looks win the first glance; evidence earns the premium.

**Overstated in the videos:**

- "Every 'did the tap work? can I go back?' is processing effort that lowers the judgement" (8EAYZe6y3I8 note). A reasonable extension, but the fluency research is about perceiving stimuli, not interaction feedback. Argue for feedback from usability evidence (visibility of system status), not from fluency.
- "Looks get you picked, evidence gets you called premium" is drawn from studies of juice and jam packaging. Directionally consistent, not shown for software.

## Consistency

**Evidence: strong as a design standard; weak as the data the video cites.** WCAG 2.2 criterion 3.2.3 (Consistent Navigation, level AA) requires repeated navigation to appear in the same relative order, and consistency is one of Nielsen's ten heuristics. The York study (Petrie et al. 2009) reported page time rising from 1.9 s to 6.8 s when the navigation moved, with users rating the impact low. It is a four page short paper whose percentages imply about 14 participants; its full text could not be read for this skill.

**Honest rule:** keep navigation, shared chrome and repeated controls in the same place, size, order and wording on every screen. Review a product as a sequence of screens side by side, not one screen at a time.

**Overstated in the videos:** "Users are about three times slower and do not notice" (8EAYZe6y3I8) presented as a finding. It is a small pilot. The rule stands on accessibility standards and usability practice, not on this one study.

## Service recovery

**Evidence: mixed.** The meta-analysis by de Matos, Henrique and Rossi (2007) found that an excellent recovery leaves satisfaction higher than no failure at all, but had no significant effect on repurchase intention, word of mouth or company image. In more than 11,000 bank customer interviews, Michel and Meuter (2008) found the paradox real but rare. PwC (2018) reports that 32% say they would leave a brand they love after one bad experience; that is a stated intention, and the US figure was 17%.

**Honest rule:** design the failure states as carefully as the happy path: say what went wrong, what the user can do, and offer the fix or contact right there (the declined card alert with a contact button, the Chrome dino page that keeps the network help next to the game). Treat recovery as damage control, not as a growth tactic.

**Overstated in the videos:**

- "When a failure is handled really well, people can end up as happy as if nothing went wrong, or happier", presented as the route to premium (8EAYZe6y3I8). True for satisfaction in some studies; loyalty and recommendations did not follow, and it is rare in field data.
- "It only takes one bad experience for about a third of people to leave". People said they would, about a brand they love, in a survey.

## Also checked

- **AI generated UI (Design Theater, Imteyaz et al. 2026): strong for what it measured.** Across 120 interfaces from five tools, about 25% of stated design rationale was not built (34% for functional requirements), and four of five tools built 6% or fewer of the functional UX principles. Rule: verify every AI built screen state by state (loading, empty, error, undo, back, disabled) against what it claims. The video cited this accurately.
- **Chrome dino:** about 270 million games a month (games, not people), from Google's own blog. Accurate.
- **Apple HIG numbers in Gfsd8NNuD9g:** 17 pt iOS body, 13 pt macOS body, 44x44 pt minimum target, five or fewer tabs. Accurate, but in points, not pixels.

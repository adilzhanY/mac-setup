# The UX Psychology Behind Apps People Can't Stop Using

- Channel: uxpeak
- URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2TlIg3VokY8
- Duration: 11:34
- Format: animated explainer. Every principle is shown as a pair of phone mockups (iPhone frame, status bar "9:41", white screen on a soft lavender gradient backdrop), a "before" and an "after", with illustrations and emoji reactions in between. The mockups are built in one consistent house style: Inter-like sans, purple accent (#7C3AED-ish violet), soft 1px borders, 12 to 16px card radii, pill buttons.

## Core thesis

Apps fail less because they look bad and more because they ignore how people actually decide. Users are not making logical decisions: defaults read as recommendations, the first number seen becomes the ruler, a gift creates a debt, building something makes it yours, and progress (even a granted head start) creates momentum. Six principles are taught, each with a concrete before/after screen: smart defaults, goal gradient effect, reciprocity, IKEA/endowment effect, loss aversion (status quo bias), and contrast effect. The designers who apply these build products people keep using; the ones who do not build products people forget.

## Extracted rules

Choice and forms
1. Never ship a form of blank fields when you can predict the answer. Pre-select the most common value for every field (date = tomorrow or today, guests = 2, seating = the usual one, time = the most popular slot).
2. Replace free-text or dropdown inputs with direct controls when the option set is small: time chips (4 visible, plus "View All Times"), a stepper for counts, icon tiles for 2 to 4 categories.
3. Mark recommended values explicitly: a "Popular times" tag on the label row, a "Most common" badge on an option.
4. Show the most likely 4 to 6 options, hide the rest behind "View all". 6 options converted 10x better than 24 in the jam study.
5. Drop fields that are not needed to get a result (the "after" booking screen removed Occasion).
6. Enable the primary button as soon as defaults make the form valid; avoid a disabled grey CTA on first paint.
7. Make the CTA label state the outcome and a concrete number: "Search 12 Available Tables", not "Search".
8. Give filled sections a visible state change (icon turns accent colour once a value exists).

Progress and onboarding
9. Never start a progress indicator at 0%. Count the steps already done (account creation, email entered) as completed steps.
10. Show progress as both a percentage and a labelled stepper (Sign up, Profile, Photo, Verify, Done) with clear states: done (filled + check), current (filled + bold label), upcoming (pale outline, grey label).
11. Pair progress with encouraging copy that shrinks the remaining work: "You're doing great! Just a few more details".
12. Treat sign-up as step 1 of onboarding, not a separate gate.

Value before asks
13. Deliver real value before asking for an account or email. Show the score, the top issues and what passed; gate only the deep version.
14. Never blur results behind "Sign up to unlock" after the user did the work to get them.
15. Phrase the ask as saving or completing what the user already has: "Want the full 50-page report? Save your results" and "Save My Report, Free". Say "Free" on the button when it is free.
16. Ask for sign-up after a completed win (end of the first lesson), framed as "Don't lose tonight's work", listing what is unsaved (score, streak, XP) with an "Unsaved" status.

Ownership
17. Let users create or customise something before sign-up (name, title, palette, card style) with a live preview card that updates as they choose.
18. Label the CTA after investment as "Continue", not "Sign up", so leaving feels like abandoning their work.
19. Follow the Duolingo order: choose (language), commit (goals), achieve (first lesson), then create account.

Framing actions
20. On upgrade or retention prompts, frame what will be lost, not what can be gained. Be concrete: the user's own file names, a count ("and 12 other"), a total ("2.8 GB will be lost"), and a deadline ("In 3 days" in bold red).
21. Write the dismiss link so the user owns the consequence ("I'll Risk It") instead of a free exit ("Maybe Later").
22. Use the danger palette consistently for loss: red warning tile, pale red inset box, red emphasis on the deadline and the total; keep the primary action in the brand colour with a protective icon (shield).

Price presentation
23. Never show an add-on price alone on its own page. Show it right after the larger anchor price it relates to (in the cart, under the product).
24. Add a relative label next to the add-on price: "Just 2.6%" (50 / 1,899) in a small green pill.
25. Put the add-on in a "Recommended for you" section between product and totals, with a compact "Add" button, not a full-page interstitial with "No thanks".
26. Decide deliberately what number the user sees first on any pricing screen; it becomes the ruler.

Visual house style visible across the "after" screens (useful as concrete values)
27. One accent colour (violet) for primary actions, selected states and progress; semantic red / amber / green reserved for status, each as a tinted icon tile plus a matching tinted 1px border on the card.
28. Hero metric pattern: small coloured label above, big bold number, smaller grey denominator ("72" then "/100"), and a status pill below ("Good, 16 issues found").
29. Section labels are small uppercase with a leading line icon; selected chips/tiles get a light accent fill plus accent border; unselected stay white with a hairline border.

## Anti-patterns and the fix

- Stack of empty fields with grey placeholders and a disabled CTA ("Blank = Friction"). Fix: smart defaults, chips, stepper, tiles, enabled CTA with outcome count.
- Too many options at once (a wall of 8+ chips, 24 jams). Fix: show the 4 to 6 most common, collapse the rest.
- Progress bar at "0% Complete" with five empty dots on the first screen. Fix: count sign-up as done, start at 20%.
- Results blurred behind "Sign up to unlock" or a locked section modal before any value. Fix: give the useful part free, gate the deep report.
- "Log in or create an account to get started" as the first screen. Fix: let them use or build something first.
- Bare "Email / Password / Sign Up" page where nothing belongs to the user. Fix: a customisation step with live preview before sign-up.
- Gain-framed upgrade modal with generic features and a "Maybe Later" escape hatch. Fix: loss-framed, specific, deadline, "I'll Risk It".
- Add-on price shown on its own page ($49.99/month alone invites the $600/year calculation). Fix: show it in the cart below the anchor, with a "% of total" label.
- Generic CTA labels ("Search", "Sign up"). Fix: outcome plus count, or "Continue" after investment.

## Quality checks before shipping

- For every input: do we know the most common answer? If yes, is it preselected?
- Is any field removable without blocking the result?
- How many options are visible at once? More than about 6 without a "view all"?
- Is the primary button enabled on first paint, and does its label say what happens and how many results?
- Does any progress indicator ever show 0%? What has the user already done that we can count?
- Does the user receive something of value before we ask for an account, email or payment?
- Is the sign-up ask framed as saving their work, listing what they would lose?
- Does the user own anything (a choice, a creation, a preview) before the account wall?
- On upgrade/retention prompts: is it gain-framed? Does it name the user's actual items, quantity and deadline? Does the dismiss copy make the cost felt?
- Is any price shown in isolation? What number does the user see right before it? Is there a relative label?

## Psychology / why it works

- Decision fatigue: each blank field is a decision; stacked decisions lead to no decision and abandonment.
- Choice overload: Columbia jam study, 24 options 3% purchase vs 6 options 30%.
- Default effect: 70 to 90% of users never change defaults; a default reads as a recommendation and social proof ("what most people pick"), so it is trust, not laziness. The job changes from creating to scanning and adjusting.
- Goal gradient effect: effort speeds up as the goal nears; perceived progress counts, so a granted head start (2 of 10 stamps) nearly doubled completion.
- Reciprocity: receiving first creates an unconscious debt to return the favour; Cialdini's strongest lever; free samples up to +2,000% purchases.
- IKEA effect: self-made things are valued more than identical ready-made ones. Endowment effect: merely feeling ownership raises value, so leaving feels like losing.
- Loss aversion: losses weigh about twice as much as equal gains (Kahneman). Status quo bias: people protect what they already have, so show the cost of inaction.
- Contrast effect / anchoring: values are judged relative to what was seen just before; a $50 add-on after $1,900 is a rounding error.
- Closing idea: users are not making logical decisions.

## Core thesis

AI tools and pattern libraries (Mobbin MCP) now let anyone ship screens that look decent in a day, so "looks good" has become the floor, which he calls the **commodity ceiling**: everything below it looks the same, like rows of identical minimalist box houses. Pretty and premium were never the same thing: research on packaging shows looks get a product picked, but evidence of a good product is what gets it called premium. The brain has no "premium detector", it quietly keeps score of every piece of evidence that the product is good, and things that are easy to process are judged more kindly. That evidence lives in three places AI leaves out: (1) the unglamorous interaction states (system status, undo, error recovery), (2) consistency between screens across months of real use, and (3) the worst case moments (failures), where great recovery can make people happier than if nothing had gone wrong.

Note on the brief's focus: this video does not discuss motion, materials, sound, haptics or visual polish as premium signals. Its argument is that premium comes from thought-through states, consistency and recovery, not from surface. Pricing appears only as the outcome ("caps how much you can charge", "what people end up paying more for").

## Extracted rules

1. Treat "looks decent" as the floor, never the goal. Anything an AI tool or pattern library can produce for everyone (gradient hero card, ring chart, emoji greeting, floating + button) does not count as premium evidence.
2. For every action in the core flow, make sure the user always knows (a) what just happened, (b) that they can take it back, (c) what to do next. Check all three per action.
3. Always show system status: a tap must visibly register, long work must show progress. Never leave the user asking "did the tap work?"
4. Give every destructive or significant action an undo or a way back.
5. Never answer user effort with nothing. If an input is not enough (too short a recording, too little data), still give something back and explain, do not just say "not enough content".
6. Write limit and quota states as facts, not slogans: show the count ("2/2 SESSIONS USED"), a plain headline ("You've used both sessions this month"), and the exact date it resets ("On September 12, your sessions will be refreshed"), with the key fact visually emphasised (darker or heavier than the surrounding text).
7. One situation, one pattern: if two screens handle the same state (out of sessions with and without upgrade), merge them into one shared component and vary only the action.
8. Keep navigation and shared chrome in exactly the same place, size and font on every screen. A moved nav bar cost 1.9 s to 6.8 s on that page and 2.2 s to 6.2 s on later pages, and users did not believe it slowed them.
9. Review the product as a whole sequence, not screen by screen: lay out all screens side by side and look for any that disagree (spacing, nav position, wording, component style).
10. Use the product for an hour like a real user and write down every spot where it stops feeling like the same product. Fix each.
11. Design failure states with the same care as success screens: offline, failed payment, empty lists, errors, declined actions.
12. On a failure, tell the user the likely cause, what to do now, and give a direct path to a human: e.g. "Your payment at Coffee Shop was declined. Please check your balance." plus a "Contact support" button on the same screen.
13. Tailor error messages to the reason (a low balance decline gets its own message), not one generic error.
14. In money or trust moments, make the failed item unmistakable but calm: strike through the amount, a small coloured status pill ("Declined"), full details (date, amount, card, transaction ID with copy), and the recovery action pinned at the bottom.
15. Where possible turn a failure into something pleasant (Chrome offline dino). Delight belongs in the worst moment, not the home screen.
16. Break the product on purpose before shipping: kill the network mid flow, fail a payment, empty every list, then design what you see.
17. For slow loads you cannot speed up, use loading and progress tactics so it feels faster (from the crypto onboarding notes: splash screen "needs some loading bar UX tactics").
18. Remove steps that a better mechanism replaces (2FA code input replaced by Face ID).
19. Reserve an accent colour for one meaning. (Demonstrated by the video's own slides: red only marks failure, cost, and missing work; everything else is black and grey.)
20. Ease of processing is a premium signal: reduce what the user has to interpret on every screen, because easy to take in is judged more kindly.

## Anti-patterns and fixes

- **Design theater**: the design rationale says status, undo and recovery are handled, but they are not in the UI (about a quarter of stated reasoning missing in the 2026 study). Fix: audit the built UI against the stated reasoning, state by state.
- **Happy case only prototypes** ("happy case, one HTML file"). Fix: enumerate loading, empty, error, offline, limit reached and declined states for every screen before calling it done.
- **The AI look**: purple gradient hero card, emoji in greetings and quick actions, ring chart, floating centre + button, tip card. Decent and interchangeable. Fix: spend the effort on states and consistency instead of decoration; earn distinctiveness in the details.
- **Slogan where information belongs**: "Keep every session within reach!" on a limit screen. Fix: factual headline, count, exact reset date.
- **Two divergent screens for one state** (one with Upgrade + Close, one with only Close). Fix: one pattern.
- **Empty outcome after effort**: "Looks like we didn't get enough content!" after a mic test. Fix: give something back, make clear it did not cost a session, offer the next step.
- **80 screens that don't quite agree**, each generated in a minute by an agent. Fix: shared components, a consistency pass across the full screen set.
- **Moving chrome** (nav position, font size, font type, order changing between pages). Fix: lock them.
- **Dead failure pages and zero information errors**: "you're just left alone". Fix: cause, next step, support button, reason specific messages.
- **Generic splash with slow load**. Fix: progress feedback that makes the wait feel shorter.

## Quality checks before shipping

- For each step of the core flow: does the user know what happened, can they undo it, do they know what's next?
- Does every tap produce visible feedback? Is there any "did that work?" moment?
- Is there any state where user effort yields nothing?
- Do all limit, quota and paywall screens show a count and an exact date?
- Lay every screen side by side: are nav, headers, spacing, fonts, buttons and wording identical where they should be? Is "screen 80" consistent with "screen 1"?
- After an hour of real use, list every place it stopped feeling like one product. Is the list empty?
- Turn off Wi-Fi mid flow. Fail a payment. Empty every list. Is each resulting state designed as carefully as the success screen?
- Does every error say why, what to do, and offer a human (support) path on the same screen?
- Is there one worst case moment that could become a "they caught me" moment?
- Would an AI tool given the same prompt produce the same thing? If yes, what evidence of care is there beyond the look?

## Psychology / why it works

- **Evidence over looks**: packaging studies (2010, 2021) show aesthetics get a product chosen, but perceived premiumness comes from evidence that the product is good. The brain has no premium detector, it keeps a quiet tally of evidence.
- **Processing fluency** (Reber, Schwarz, Winkielman 2004): things that are easy to perceive and process are judged as more beautiful and more positively. Every "did the tap work? can I go back?" is processing effort that lowers the judgement.
- **People misreport friction**: in the York study most participants noticed the nav move, rated its effect low (all mean ratings below the midpoint of a 1 to 5 scale), yet were 3x slower. So inconsistency costs more than users say and more than analytics show.
- **Service recovery paradox** (2007 review): an excellently handled failure can leave people as satisfied as, or more satisfied than, no failure at all. Failure moments are the highest leverage place to create the premium feeling.
- **One bad experience** is enough for about a third of people to leave (PwC 2018), so churn comes from bad moments, not from the home screen.
- **Commoditisation**: whatever an AI tool learns, every competitor gets the same day, so tool improvements raise the floor, not your position. Only product specific thinking (states, connective tissue) stays yours.

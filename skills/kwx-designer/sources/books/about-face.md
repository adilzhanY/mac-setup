## Key lessons

1. **Interaction design is the design of behaviour,** not only form (introduction). The old oven had one knob: every position always meant the same thing. The digital oven's buttons (Start, Cancel, Program) do different things depending on state and sequence. That is "complex behaviour", and taming it is the job.
2. **Goal-Directed Design:** design for the reasons people use a product (their goals, expectations, attitudes and aptitudes), understood as narratives, not for a list of atomic requirements. Gestalt: a product must be designed as a whole in its context.
3. **Posture** (chapter 9 heading; concept from Wikipedia and general knowledge): how much of the user's attention a product holds. Sovereign apps hold attention for long sessions (an editor, an IDE) and can be dense, with stable layouts and accelerators. Transient apps appear briefly for one job and leave (a volume popup, a colour picker) and must be instantly readable, large and simple. Daemonic apps run unseen in the background. Match density and learnability to the posture.
4. **Perpetual intermediates** (chapter 10 heading "Optimizing for Intermediates"; concept): most users never stay beginners and never become experts. Design the main path for the intermediate, make beginners' first steps easy, and give experts accelerators.
5. **Excise** (chapter 12 "Reducing Work and Eliminating Excise"; concept): work the tool demands that does not serve the user's goal (navigating between windows, setting up, confirming, waiting, re-entering data). Goal-directed tasks versus excise tasks: eliminate excise. Bret Victor applies this directly: navigation in information software is "pure excise".
6. Other chapters named in the contents that this skill draws on as ideas: "Preventing Errors and Informing Decisions" (rich modeless feedback; undo, redo and reversible histories; compare and preview), "Metaphors, Idioms, and Affordances" (idioms are learned once and then work; escape the grip of metaphor), "Digital Etiquette" (considerate products).

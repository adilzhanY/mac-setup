# Don't Make Me Think, Revisited (Steve Krug, 3rd edition 2014)

## What was read, and where

- Publisher sample pages (Peachpit / New Riders via Cisco Press): https://www.ciscopress.com/content/images/9780321965516/samplepages/0321965515.pdf
  Contents of the sample: front matter, the preface "About this edition", all of chapter 4 "Animal, Vegetable, or Mineral? Why users like mindless choices", and the index (read in full via pdftotext).
- The table of contents in the sample and the O'Reilly listing show the other chapter themes (chapter 1 "Don't make me think!", chapter 2 scanning, satisficing and muddling through, chapter 3 "Billboard Design 101"). Those chapter ideas are well known and are summarised from them, not quoted.

## Key lessons

1. **Krug's first law: don't make me think.** Each screen should be self-evident; failing that, self-explanatory.
2. **Krug's second law (chapter 4, read in full):** "It doesn't matter how many times I have to click, as long as each click is a mindless, unambiguous choice." Rule of thumb: "three mindless, unambiguous clicks equal one click that requires thought." What counts is the thought and uncertainty per click, plus continued confidence that the user is on the right track (the scent of information).
3. **Mindless choices:** "Animal, vegetable or mineral?" needs almost no thought. Hard choices ("Home office" or "Small business"? "Subscriber" or "Member"?) make users question whether they want the thing at all. The New York Times example defers detail: first log in or subscribe, then only the relevant questions.
4. **When a choice cannot be made simple, give guidance that is brief, timely and unavoidable.** Examples: tips beside form fields, "What's this?" links, tooltips. The model is the London kerb marking "LOOK RIGHT" with an arrow: brief, seen at the instant of need, impossible to miss.
5. **People scan, satisfice and muddle through** (chapter 2 theme): design so the first plausible option is correct.
6. **Billboard design** (chapter 3 theme): use conventions, a clear visual hierarchy, clearly defined areas, obvious clickability, and low noise.
7. **"Usability is about people and how they understand and use things, not about technology."** And, quoting Nielsen: what was difficult for users twenty years ago is still difficult today.
8. Index shows coverage of affordances and signifiers (pp. 151 to 153), flat design, the trunk test (pp. 82 to 83), the reservoir of goodwill (pp. 166 to 171), and omitting needless words.

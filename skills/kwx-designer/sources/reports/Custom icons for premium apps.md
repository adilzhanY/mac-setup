# Draw the icons your pack cannot say

A premium app should own its icon system, but it rarely needs to draw every glyph from nothing. The products with the most admired iconography (Apple, Figma, Atlassian, Uber, Spotify, GitHub, Raycast, Shopify, IBM) all draw their own sets, and their published reasons are concrete rather than vague talk of "brand": icons that share the typeface's weight and vertical metrics, metaphors for domain concepts no pack ships, a clear filled versus outline state language, and an end to the drift that sets in when teams mix sources. Several of these sets started as packs or as piecemeal growth and were redrawn later, which shows the real decision is about the specification, not the provenance: one canvas and padding, one stroke matched to the stem of the body text, one corner rule, one state rule. For most teams the right answer is a hybrid that Apple's own guidelines endorse, with conventional metaphors for shared verbs (search, close, share, delete) tuned to that spec, and custom drawings where the product's own nouns live. The cost is coverage and governance more than drawing time: Uber redrew 1,500 icons in under four months, and Atlassian had to migrate more than 16,000 call sites. On the web, deliver icons as inline or component SVG, because icon fonts blur, break under font overrides and ad blockers, and carry poor semantics; inside native toolkits that bundle their own font, such as Qt/QML, a font stays cheap and robust. An AI agent can draw a set that holds up by writing named geometric primitives on a 24 unit grid under a fixed root element, then verifying each icon by rasterising it and reading the pixels instead of trusting its eye. One honest caveat frames all of this: no user research or A/B data was found that measures whether users notice or prefer custom icons over stock ones, so the case rests on craft, coherence and domain fit, not on proven preference.

## Large products redraw their icons to cure drift and to match their type

The strongest pattern in the case studies is not "premium apps start custom". It is that products grow until their icons drift, and the cure is a redrawn set tied to the typeface, with rules and tooling. **Atlassian's legacy library held 350+ icons built over six years with "inconsistent sizing, spacing, and metaphors"**, and an audit found **over 1,000 custom icons** drawn by teams in isolated repos, some carrying different meanings for the same glyph across products ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/design/behind-the-screens-building-atlassians-new-icon-system)). Uber's old set used 3px outlines and sharp corners, and at small sizes "There simply weren't enough pixels to express complex ideas with precision"; filled icons became the default, but because the brand colour is black, the selected state moved from grey to black, a change many users could not see ([Uber Design](https://uber.design/when-to-evolve-a-design-system)). Its new set is built on the Uber Move typeface as "two expressions of one visual alphabet": a 2px stroke, square caps "to match the precision of the typeface", exaggerated roundness and a defined filled and outline relationship, with **1,500 icons redrawn in under four months** ([Uber Design](https://uber.design/when-to-evolve-a-design-system)).

Apple is the purest example of icons treated as part of the type system. SF Symbols "come in weights that match our family of system fonts", are specified in typographic points, sit on the text baseline, and "Every weight has been drawn manually", with stroke thickness adjusted per weight rather than scaled linearly ([WWDC19 session 206](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/206/)). The current HIG lists **nine weights matched to San Francisco and three scales defined against its cap height** ([HIG SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols)). GitHub drew Octicons because existing icons had "metaphors that just barely fit what we needed them to" ([GitHub blog](https://github.blog/news-insights/say-hello-to-octicons/)), and Figma's UI3 shipped **200 icons hand drawn by Tim Van Damme** as "visual explanations of how to interact with the platform", after its earlier abstract icons grew "harder to parse" as controls multiplied ([Figma blog](https://www.figma.com/blog/behind-our-redesign-ui3/)). Raycast is the clearest primary example of the path from packs to custom: its previous set "combined icons from packs and custom variations", and the 400+ icon redesign with James McDonald set stroke and radius rules that "unblocked us from creating new icons at speed" ([Raycast blog](https://www.raycast.com/blog/launch-week-summary)).

Two motives recur across these stories. The first is typeface coherence: Apple matches SF weights and cap height, Uber matches Uber Move's terminals, and Atlassian tuned its stroke to "the stroke of standard UI text" ([Atlassian Design](https://atlassian.design/whats-new/building-atlassians-new-icon-system)). An icon set that shares stroke weight, terminal shape and vertical metrics with the UI font is what reads as one product. The second is domain metaphor: no pack ships "pull request", "auto layout" or a Linear style issue state, and Linear says it relies on icons "to make projects, issues, initiatives, and statuses recognizable at a glance" ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/behind-the-latest-design-refresh)). Linear's March 2026 refresh also carries the counterweight: it found icon use had become excessive, so it **reduced icon usage, scaled sizes down and removed coloured team icon backgrounds** ([Linear changelog](https://linear.app/changelog/2026-03-12-ui-refresh)). Restraint is part of the premium look. Expressive brand iconography is a separate track: Airbnb's 2025 move to 3D, animated category icons was done "by an in-house team" ([It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/airbnb-app-redesign-140525)), but critics found the hyper-realistic icons so specific they detract from their message ([Design Bootcamp](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/airbnb-summer-2025-update-heres-what-s-new-and-why-it-matters-0ced2338b921)), and none of it replaces utility glyphs.

The redraws also carry a learnability bill. Figma admitted that an icon-only appearance control slowed workflows because users had to wait for a tooltip to learn the blend mode ([Figma blog](https://www.figma.com/blog/our-approach-to-designing-ui3/)), and forum users complained they "had to re-learn the icons and sections" ([Figma forum](https://forum.figma.com/share-your-feedback-26/ui3-feedback-3058/index14.html)). Several gaps remain open: Stripe, Notion, Dropbox, Duolingo and Arc have published no primary writeup on their product icons, and no source says whether Linear's general glyphs are drawn in house.

## A 24 grid, a stem matched stroke and one radius rule make a family

### Canvas, live area and keylines

The dominant convention is a **24 unit canvas with a 20 unit live area (2 units of padding) and four keyline shapes**: Material's circle of 20, square of 18, portrait rectangle 16 wide by 20 high, and landscape rectangle 20 wide by 16 high ([Material Design, System icons](https://material.io/design/iconography/system-icons.html)). The circle is drawn about 11% wider than the square because circles look smaller at equal size, which is optical balance encoded as numbers ([Adobe Design, Part 2](https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-design-effective-icons-part-2)). Lucide only requires 1px of padding ([Lucide icon design guide](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icon-design-guide)), yet its house icon spans x=3 to x=21, so with a 2px stroke the ink lands at 2 to 22, the same 2px visual padding as Material ([lucide house.svg](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lucide-icons/lucide/main/icons/house.svg)). Smaller systems draw on smaller grids: Atlassian draws "within a 16 x 16px bounding box" ([Atlassian Design](https://atlassian.design/whats-new/building-atlassians-new-icon-system)), Polaris uses a 20px container with a 16px safe area ([Polaris](https://polaris.shopify.com/design/icons/creating-icons)), and IBM draws on a 32px artboard with 2px padding and scales down ([Carbon v10](https://v10.carbondesignsystem.com/guidelines/icons/contribute)). Phosphor ships a 256 unit viewBox with 32 units of padding per side, equal to 3px at 24px, so Phosphor glyphs read slightly smaller than Lucide at the same nominal size ([Phosphor house.svg](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/phosphor-icons/core/main/raw/regular/house.svg)).

### Stroke weight follows the text stem

Outline systems split into two camps: **2px on a 24 grid** (Material, Lucide, Tabler, legacy Atlassian) and **1.5px on 16 or 20** (Octicons, new Atlassian, Polaris). The second camp exists because it matches small text: GitLab estimates that most regular sans serif system fonts have stems of about 1.5px at 16px ([GitLab design issue 1432](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab-design/-/issues/1432)), and Atlassian chose 1.5px on 16 for exactly that reason. The arithmetic links the camps: a 2px stroke on a 24 grid rendered at 16 to 18px lands at about 1.3 to 1.5px on screen. Two scaling philosophies follow. Lucide and Phosphor scale the stroke with the canvas, while Octicons holds **1.5px constant at both 16 and 24** so either size sits next to the same text weight ([Primer](https://primer.style/foundations/icons/design-guidelines)). Caps and joins are a style decision with brand weight: round caps and joins dominate outline sets, Atlassian and Uber deliberately chose square caps to echo their typefaces, and IBM forbids centred strokes entirely, expanding every stroke to fills at whole pixel values because "Centering can cause half pixels" ([Carbon v10](https://v10.carbondesignsystem.com/guidelines/icons/contribute)).

| System | Canvas | Padding | Stroke | Caps and joins | Corner radius | Minimum gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Material | 24 | 2 | 2 (1.5 optical exceptions) | not specified | 2 outer, square inner | not specified |
| Lucide | 24 | at least 1 (2 in practice) | 2, never mixed | round | 2 on shapes 8+ wide, 1 below, 2.41 at diagonal right angles | 2 |
| Octicons | 16 and 24, drawn separately | not published | 1.5 at both sizes | round | 1 (0.25 on small fills) | 1, or 1.5 around modifiers |
| Atlassian 2025 | 16 (12 downscaled) | not published | 1.5 | rounded forms, square end caps | follows the typeface | not published |
| Polaris | 20 | 2 | 1.5 (1.25 for cut-outs) | not published | not published | 1 |
| IBM Carbon | 32, scaled down | 2 | 2, expanded to fills | not applicable | 2 on rounded shapes, square where the object is square | not published |
| Phosphor Regular | 256 units | 32 units | 16 units (1.5px at 24) | round | 8 units | not published |

### Corners, gaps and curves converge on one stroke width

Corner rules converge on a radius of about one stroke width. Lucide gives **2px on shapes at least 8px across, 1px on smaller shapes, and 2.41px (1 plus the square root of 2) where diagonals meet at a right angle**, notes that "Even very sharp corners usually need a small amount of rounding", and forbids rounding "where more than two lines meet" ([Lucide](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icon-design-guide)). Material takes the sharper path: 2dp radius on exterior silhouette corners, square interior corners, and no rounding on strokes of 2dp or less ([Material 1](https://m1.material.io/style/icons.html)). Either works if it applies everywhere. Gaps scale with the stroke: Lucide requires at least 2px between distinct elements and inside inner gaps, tested by fitting a 2px circle, while 1.5px systems allow 1px ([Lucide](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icon-design-guide); [Polaris](https://polaris.shopify.com/design/icons/creating-icons)). Lucide also prefers simple arcs and quadratic curves over cubic Beziers and asks for control points aligned to avoid bumps, which matters as much for hand written code as for a vector editor. IBM restricts angles to multiples of 15 degrees ([IBM Design Language](https://www.ibm.com/design/language/iconography/ui-icons/design/)); the popular "45 degrees only" rule was not found in any primary source.

### Pixel snapping decides how 16 and 20px look

Every system that cares about crispness snaps geometry to integers. Material puts icons "on pixel" ([Material 1](https://m1.material.io/style/icons.html)), Carbon allows "No decimals" in coordinates or sizes ([Carbon v10](https://v10.carbondesignsystem.com/guidelines/icons/contribute)), and Lucide aligns coordinates, arc centres and diagonals to the grid "when possible" ([Lucide](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icon-design-guide)). The underlying geometry is simple: a 1px line straddling a pixel boundary renders as two 50% grey lines ([WHATWG list](https://lists.whatwg.org/pipermail/whatwg-whatwg.org/2013-July/082515.html)), so even width strokes belong on whole coordinates and odd width strokes on half coordinates. A 1.5px stroke can never have both edges on whole pixels, which is why Polaris aligns one side of the stroke to the grid and Octicons aligns outer edges only ([Polaris](https://polaris.shopify.com/design/icons/creating-icons); [Primer](https://primer.style/foundations/icons/design-guidelines)).

A reproducible rasterisation test run for this report (resvg 0.48.1 and ImageMagick 7.1.2, not a published source) makes the consequence concrete. A 2px stroke on an integer coordinate rendered at 24px gave an alpha profile of `0 0 255 255 0 0`, two perfectly solid pixels; the same line moved to x=11.5 smeared to `0 128 255 128 0 0`. More important, **the share of anti-aliased ink pixels was 16% at 24px but 67% at 16px and 71% at 20px**, because a 24 grid scaled by 0.667 or 0.833 puts every edge on a fraction. No coordinate choice on a 24 master is crisp at 16 or 20. This is why the systems that care about small sizes draw separate masters: Octicons draws every icon at 16 and at 24 ([Primer](https://primer.style/foundations/icons/design-guidelines)), Fluent ships per size files at 16, 20, 24, 28 and 48 ([@fluentui/svg-icons](https://npmjs.com/package/@fluentui/svg-icons)), Spotify produced each icon in five sizes ([Hypebot](https://www.hypebot.com/a-look-inside-une-spotify-redesign-process/)), and Atlassian removed the ability to scale icons after developers distorted stroke and padding with a size prop ([Atlassian RFC-81](https://community.developer.atlassian.com/t/rfc-81-upcoming-changes-to-iconography/87753)). The same arithmetic applies to fractional UI scaling: at a scale of 1.1, a 24px icon renders at 26.4px and anti-aliases regardless of how carefully it was snapped, so design time snapping survives only at integer scale factors.

### Weights, optical sizes and filled states

Optical sizing means thinner relative strokes at larger sizes so big icons do not look heavy. Material Symbols encodes this as variable font axes: `wght` 100 to 700, `opsz` 20 to 48 (which "automatically adjust[s] the stroke weight"), `GRAD` for weight changes that keep metrics fixed, useful to calm light icons on dark backgrounds, and `FILL` 0 to 1 for state ([Google Fonts](https://developers.google.com/fonts/docs/material_symbols)). Google's page states the default `opsz` as 48 in one place and 24 in another, and a package reports `GRAD` from -25 rather than -50 ([Fontsource](https://fontsource.org/docs/getting-started/material-symbols.md)), so check values before relying on defaults. Apple's custom symbol templates need **three source drawings, Ultralight, Regular and Black at small scale**, from which the other weights interpolate ([Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/custom-sf-symbols-creation)), and a custom symbol must provide at least regular at medium scale plus bold variants for Bold Text ([WWDC19](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/206/)).

The state convention is close to universal: **outline at rest, fill for selected or emphasised**. SF Symbols uses outline in toolbars and lists and fill in tab bars and swipe actions ([HIG mirror](https://unpkg.com/apple-hig-mcp@1.1.3/content/sf-symbols.md)), Fluent uses Regular for wayfinding and Filled for selection ([Fluent 2](https://fluent2.microsoft.design/iconography)), and Polaris fills "semi-permanent selected states, like pinning an app" ([Polaris](https://polaris.shopify.com/design/icons/creating-icons)). A filled twin must keep the outline's silhouette so the change reads as a fill, not a swap, and Uber's failure shows the state must also survive the brand colour: a selected state that differs only in tone from its resting state is invisible to many users ([Uber Design](https://uber.design/when-to-evolve-a-design-system)). No source gives a rule for knock-out gaps inside filled shapes beyond Polaris' 1.25px cut-out stroke.

### Metaphor and perspective stay literal and front facing

Material demands front facing icons that are never tilted or made dimensional ([Material 1](https://m1.material.io/style/icons.html)); Octicons and Windows allow depth only when the object is unrecognisable without it, such as the top of a cylinder ([Primer](https://primer.style/foundations/icons/design-guidelines); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/apps/design/style/iconography/app-icon-design)). Windows caps an icon at two metaphors and prefers one. Fluent names system icons for the object, "Shield, not security" ([Fluent 2](https://fluent2.microsoft.design/iconography)), and Atlassian names single purpose icons by concept and multi purpose icons by appearance ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/design/behind-the-screens-building-atlassians-new-icon-system)). NN/g's research tempers ambition here: **only home, print and the magnifying glass for search proved widely recognised**, heart and star mean different things on different sites, and if a designer needs more than five seconds to think of an icon for a concept, the concept needs a label instead ([NN/g, Icon Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)).

## Seven steps take a set from audit to a contact sheet

The process shared by Adobe, Atlassian, Lucide, Streamline and NN/g follows one arc: inventory the need, choose familiar metaphors, freeze the rules, prove them on a small set in the real interface, then expand by reuse and review every addition against the family at real size. The order matters most at step five, because Atlassian only discovered that its bold brand shapes overpowered the product UI once they sat in the actual apps ([Atlassian Design](https://atlassian.design/whats-new/building-atlassians-new-icon-system)).

| Step | What happens | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Audit | List every concept the product needs and every icon already shipped; Atlassian built an extractor that screenshots every SVG in its repos for review | [Atlassian Design](https://atlassian.design/whats-new/building-atlassians-new-icon-system) |
| 2. Brief and metaphor | Name the task each icon helps with, research how others draw it, keep familiar metaphors and never use a familiar shape such as an arrow in an unfamiliar way | [Adobe, Part 1](https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-design-effective-icons-part-1), [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/) |
| 3. Write the spec | Fix grid, padding, stroke, caps, radius, gap, keyshapes, and plan the badge cut-out and position before drawing | [Adobe, Part 1](https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-design-effective-icons-part-1), [Adobe, Part 2](https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-design-effective-icons-part-2) |
| 4. Seed set | Draw reference circle and square plus icons that exercise every rule: a badge, a diagonal, an inner gap, an organic form, a dense icon | [Lucide](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icon-design-guide) (references); combination is synthesis |
| 5. Test in context | Place the seed set beside real type and buttons, compare style options in the product | [Atlassian Design](https://atlassian.design/whats-new/building-atlassians-new-icon-system), [Adobe, Part 2](https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-design-effective-icons-part-2) |
| 6. Expand by reuse | Reuse base shapes unchanged in geometry and placement, keep badges consistent, keep a live stroke master and export outlined copies | [Lucide](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icon-design-guide), [Adobe, Part 2](https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-design-effective-icons-part-2) |
| 7. Test and review | Recognition, findability and family tests, blur and squint checks, contrast checks with a tool | [NN/g, Icon Testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-testing/) |

No primary source prescribes a seed set size; the common "five to ten icons" advice is workshop lore. The documented core of the idea is Lucide's `circle` and `square` reference icons, which every new icon is blurred against to match visual weight, and Adobe's keyshapes that set proportions for the whole set. Adobe adds two craft habits worth keeping: edit at 3200% while a second window shows the icon at 100%, and keep early duplicates, because the first version of an organic form is sometimes the best ([Adobe, Part 1](https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-design-effective-icons-part-1)).

Testing has four distinct jobs, and NN/g separates them. Recognition shows the icon alone and asks what it is; if guesses miss, the icon is abandoned rather than tweaked. Information scent adds minimal context and asks what will happen on click. Findability places the icon in the full interface and measures time and first click accuracy, where wrong first clicks mean icons look too alike. Attractiveness shows the whole family and asks which icon is liked most and least, which catches the one or two weak members of a set ([NN/g, Icon Testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-testing/)). Craft checks sit alongside: Lucide's blur test for crowded areas, Atlassian's squint test for family resemblance, Adobe's glance test of looking away and back, and its warning to check contrast with a tool, "Do not rely on your perception" ([Adobe, Part 2](https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-design-effective-icons-part-2)). NN/g's standing recommendation is a visible text label even on standard icons, since hover labels fail on touch ([NN/g, Icon Usability](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/icon-usability/)).

Consistency over time comes from four layers that large sets all converge on. A written guide with numbers comes first. Then templates and reference shapes. Then automated lint on the source: Lucide checks a 24 viewBox, `fill="none"`, `stroke="currentColor"`, default stroke attributes, three decimal precision, an allowed element list, and bans transforms, filters, child colours and `<use>`, plus a schema validated JSON sidecar with tags and categories ([Lucide code conventions](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icons/code-conventions); [Lucide metadata](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icons/metadata-conventions)). Last comes a human ritual with one approver: Streamline's designers post daily, an art director gives final approval and reviews whole sets, and at most two designers make corrections before export ([Streamline](https://blog.streamlinehq.com/evaluating-icons/)). Atlassian's goal was a set that "feels as if it were designed by one hand", and its main lesson was "Technical tooling matters as much as visual design" ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/design/behind-the-screens-building-atlassians-new-icon-system)). Naming choices differ by purpose: Lucide names the picture ("floppy-disk", not "save") because it is a general library ([Lucide naming](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icons/naming-conventions)), while a product set where each icon has one job benefits from Atlassian's split, so a product can name files by picture and tag them by meaning.

The common mistakes are all invisible to an icon reviewed alone at large zoom and obvious in a contact sheet at real size. Noun Project names small variations in stroke weight as the classic fault ([Noun Project](https://blog.thenounproject.com/?p=8558)). Others are detail that turns to mush at 16px, circles that look small beside squares, dense icons that look darker, near duplicate base shapes, thin outlines that look "frail", excess anchor points that cause rounding errors ([Adobe, Part 2](https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-design-effective-icons-part-2)), and hard coded colours that break theming ([Lucide](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icons/code-conventions)). That is why every source ends up recommending a family view.

## Packs win for prototypes, shared verbs and native Apple apps

The steelman for packs is strong. A pack gives thousands of glyphs on day one, while a custom set gives dozens and a permanent backlog, since every new feature needs a new icon in the same style forever. Users already know pack and platform metaphors, and redrawing "trash" or "share" in a new form buys little distinction and costs learnability. Platform glyphs also get free upgrades: when iOS 13 turned system icons into symbols, apps using them "will automatically use the new designs" ([WWDC19](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2019/206/)), and system colours on SF Symbols adapt automatically to accessibility settings, vibrancy and Dark Mode ([HIG SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols)). Custom art takes on all of those obligations, including alternative text labels for every custom icon and, for custom symbols, layer annotation and testing under every animation preset ([HIG Icons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/icons)). Packs are also more tunable than their critics admit: Lucide exposes `strokeWidth`, `absoluteStrokeWidth` and a global provider ([Lucide docs](https://lucide.dev/guide/react/basics/stroke-width)), and Material Symbols exposes weight, fill, grade and optical size in outlined, rounded and sharp styles ([Material blog](https://m3.material.io/blog/introducing-symbols)).

The "looks like every AI app" critique of stock packs is real but thin on evidence. Critics name Lucide and Heroicons as the default picks in generated interfaces, with a generic stroke everywhere and the "icon in a tinted rounded square on every feature card" pattern ([UX Skill](https://uxskill.laithjunaidy.com/blog/ai-icon-design-generic.html); [Mowgli](https://mowgli.ai/blog/why-ai-built-apps-look-the-same)), but these are designer opinions, not research. Read closely, the generic signal comes less from the glyphs than from using them decoratively and untuned, which Linear's move to fewer, smaller icons addresses from the other side.

Apple's HIG settles the hybrid question directly: "Whether you use only custom icons or mix custom and system-provided ones, all interface icons in your app need to use a consistent size, level of detail, stroke thickness (or weight), and perspective", and "match the weights of interface icons and adjacent text" ([HIG Icons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/icons)). Apple's own custom symbol workflow is the hybrid in miniature: export the template of a similar symbol and modify it ([HIG SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols)). Material makes the same split between system icons (use Material Symbols or draw on its keylines) and product icons, which are always "the visual expression of a brand and product" ([Material 1](https://m1.material.io/style/icons.html)). Licences can force the custom route: SF Symbols may not appear in app icons, logos or any trademarked use, and Polaris icons are licensed only for apps that integrate with Shopify ([HIG SF Symbols](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/sf-symbols); [npm @shopify/polaris-icons](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@shopify/polaris-icons)).

| Situation | Choice |
|---|---|
| Prototype, features not yet stable | A pack, tuned once (stroke, size, corner style) to the UI font |
| Generic verbs: close, search, chevrons, share, delete, settings | The conventional metaphor from the pack or platform, which Apple publishes as standard icons ([HIG Icons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/icons)) |
| Native Apple app | SF Symbols by default, custom symbols from SF templates only for missing concepts |
| Domain and brand nouns the pack lacks or approximates poorly | Draw custom, on the pack's grid and stroke so the two halves are indistinguishable |
| Many surfaces, several teams, or a public extension API | A fully owned, documented set with naming rules, lint and migration tooling, because drift is the failure mode |

The budget is where teams misjudge. Published numbers are team scale: Uber's 1,500 icons sat inside a project of about a year with naming, guidelines and design to code tooling, and Atlassian needed an ESLint rule with an auto fixer (about 75% of 350 old icons migrated safely), a runtime "icon facade", feature flags and a custom Figma plugin for more than 16,000 call sites ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/design/behind-the-screens-building-atlassians-new-icon-system)). No company published a threshold in icon count or team size for when to move from pack to custom, and no per icon hour figures exist.

## SVG beats icon fonts on the web, fonts survive where they ship with the app

On the web, the evidence favours SVG on every axis a premium product cares about. GitHub dropped its Octicons font in 2016 because font icons "often looked poor on 1x displays" and blurred in WebKit depending on window width, while SVG renders as an image, "locking nicely to whole pixel values at any resolution"; users who override page fonts, including some dyslexic users, saw empty squares, and the font caused a flash of missing icons while it loaded ([GitHub Engineering](https://github.blog/engineering/architecture-optimization/delivering-octicons-with-svg/)). GitHub also gained size appropriate drawings, since the font had to ship one 16px glyph at every size. CSS-Tricks adds that a font's box depends on line height, vertical align and glyph metrics, and lists CORS failures, ad blockers that block custom fonts, and Private Use Area code points rendering as emoji; ad blockers do not touch inline SVG ([CSS-Tricks](https://css-tricks.com/icon-fonts-vs-svg/)). A ligature font such as Material Symbols writes the icon name as real text, so by how ligatures work, a screen reader reads "home" aloud unless the element is hidden, and the raw word appears when the font is blocked; no primary test of this was found.

Accessibility has a tested pattern. Sara Soueidan's recommendation, checked by Scott O'Hara across browser and screen reader pairs, is to hide the graphic and name the button with text: `<button><svg aria-hidden="true" focusable="false">...</svg><span class="visually-hidden">Menu</span></button>`, or `aria-label` on the button itself. Putting `aria-label` on the svg, or `role="img"` with a `<title>` to name a button, fails in some combinations, and the label should describe the action, not the picture ([Sara Soueidan](https://sarasoueidan.com/blog/accessible-icon-buttons/)).

Performance is a wash once icons are optimised, and the only failure is bloated inline markup. Cloud Four rendered 1,000 icons per technique: **inline SVG averaged 74.91ms optimised but 191.89ms unoptimised**, img with a data URI 67.24ms, an external sprite 126.04ms, and CSS masks were generally the slowest at 148.87ms ([Cloud Four](https://cloudfour.com/thinks/svg-icon-stress-test/)). That 2021 benchmark is the newest found, and engines have changed since. Optimisation with SVGO v4 is safe by default because `removeViewBox` left `preset-default` "to preserve scalability" ([SVGO migration](https://svgo.dev/docs/migrations/migration-from-v3-to-v4/)); keep an unoptimised source folder, ship about three decimals of precision, and theme with `stroke="currentColor"` or `fill="currentColor"` on the root, as GitHub did so CSS could set colour. `vector-effect="non-scaling-stroke"` keeps stroke width fixed on screen regardless of scale ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/SVG/Reference/Attribute/vector-effect)), which is right for non uniformly stretched shapes and wrong as a substitute for drawing at the target size.

Fonts keep a real place where the app bundles the font and text shaping is the cheapest renderer. In Qt/QML, a glyph gets text anti-aliasing, follows `color` like text, scales with the font size token and needs no per icon file; the trade-off is one colour per glyph and no per part animation. Qt's SVG support covers static SVG Tiny 1.2 with additions since 6.7 (masks, symbol, marker, pattern, some filters) but **does not support `clipPath`** ([Qt docs](https://doc.qt.io/qt-6/svgextensions.html)), and Qt 6.8's `VectorImage` builds a Quick scene from an SVG, though the Qt blog says a plain `Image` is often right for a single size ([Qt blog](https://www.qt.io/blog/vector-graphics-in-qt-6.8)). Material Symbols on the web is worth its cost only when its axes animate fill or weight, and then Google recommends subsetting with `&icon_names=` because the full axis set "increases the payload" ([Google Fonts](https://developers.google.com/fonts/docs/material_symbols)). Each native platform wants its own container from one plain SVG source: Android VectorDrawable or Compose ImageVector, which keep strokes natively ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/graphics/vector-drawable-resources)); react-native-svg components with `currentColor` resolved at build time, since runtime `currentColor` on a Path warns ([react-native-svg-transformer](https://docsearch.algolia.com/mcp/docs/repo/kristerkari/react-native-svg-transformer); [issue mirror](https://lightrun.com/answers/software-mansion-react-native-svg-currentcolor-fill-support)); and SF Symbol templates exported, edited and validated in the SF Symbols app ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technologyoverviews/custom-sf-symbols.md)). Templates work with filled outlines, so stroke sources must be expanded before import, and a forum report shows Xcode 16.2 rejecting Template 6.0 files ([Apple forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/690608)).

## An agent draws good icons with primitives and a raster check

The research on LLMs and SVG points to one method. General models write valid SVG but their ability is "inconsistent, especially when dealing with intricate vector compositions", reasoning improves scores, and even strong models struggle with edits that need precise syntax ([survey, arXiv 2503.04983](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04983v2)). The methods that improved results map onto an agent loop without training. Chat2SVG has the LLM write a template of basic geometric primitives first, because LLMs lay out scenes well but "struggle with complex geometric shapes" ([Chat2SVG](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.16602v1)). RLRF renders candidate SVGs and rewards pixel similarity, semantic alignment and code efficiency, which "significantly outperforms supervised fine-tuning" ([RLRF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20793)). Simon Willison's pelican on a bicycle test is not a guide: he now says its link to model quality "has been mostly severed" ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Apr/7/glm-51/)), and it rewards scene composition, not grid discipline. No controlled experiment on a general model drawing grid conformant UI icons with render feedback was found, so the method below is synthesis from these findings and Lucide's published rules.

The agent should write the plan in words before any code, naming the metaphor and each part as a primitive with numbers ("padlock: body rect 18 by 11 at y=11 with rx 2, shackle as an arc"), and check it against the nearest existing icon in the reference set, reusing that icon's base geometry exactly. The root element never varies: `<svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" width="24" height="24" viewBox="0 0 24 24" fill="none" stroke="currentColor" stroke-width="2" stroke-linecap="round" stroke-linejoin="round">`, with children carrying geometry only ([Lucide code conventions](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icons/code-conventions)). With a 2px stroke, lines and rect edges sit on integer coordinates inside 3 to 21, so ink stays in the 2 to 22 live area. Element choice follows readability for the model: `rect` with `rx` for boxes, `circle` for round parts, `line` and `polyline` for straight and bent strokes, and `path` only for arcs and curves, using absolute commands and preferring `A` and `Q` to `C`. Parts keep 2px gaps, and where one part crosses another, the agent cuts a gap instead of overlapping strokes.

Verification replaces the eye. Rasterising with resvg at 16, 20, 24 and 48px and reading pixels with ImageMagick covers most of what a designer checks visually. `magick out24.png -alpha extract -trim info:-` reports the ink box in one line (the test icon gave `18x18+3+3`, inside the live area); a single row profile shows whether a stroke is crisp; the share of partial pixels at 24px flags off grid geometry (the test gave 16% for clean integer geometry against 25% with one half pixel line, a starting threshold rather than a sourced one); and `resvg --query-all` lists each element's geometric bounds, to which half the stroke width must be added. A short XML lint can then enforce the root attributes, the allowed elements, three decimal precision and no child stroke, fill or transform, which guarantees a uniform stroke by construction. Counting connected components at 48px and again at 16px catches gaps that close at small sizes, comparing average ink coverage with the reference circle and square approximates Lucide's blur test, and dumping the 24px alpha as a 24 by 24 grid of characters gives the model a picture it can actually read. Finally, every new icon goes onto a contact sheet with its family, because the research agrees that consistency faults only show in the group.

## Conclusion

The question "custom or pack" turns out to be the wrong axis. Every redraw in this research was triggered by breaking one of four rules (canvas and padding, stroke matched to the text stem, corner radius, state contrast), never by the mere use of a pack, and Raycast and Apple both build custom glyphs by starting from existing ones. The useful principle is to borrow the verbs and own the nouns: users already know what search and delete look like, but a product's own objects and states are where a pack's metaphors "just barely fit", where the user looks most often, and where drawing pays back. Restraint is part of the same discipline, since Linear's newest refresh earned its calm look by removing and shrinking icons rather than adding them.

Two implications follow for anyone building such a system now. First, small size crispness is a delivery decision as much as a drawing decision: a 24 master can never be sharp at 16 or 20px, and fractional interface scaling breaks snapping at any size, so teams either draw separate small masters or accept soft edges deliberately. A shell that maps a pack such as Lucide into a font and adds its own glyphs on Lucide's grid is the hybrid in its cleanest form, as long as the new glyphs pass the same raster checks. Second, for an AI agent the bottleneck is verification, not generation. A model that plans in primitives and checks its output with a renderer and a lint script can hold a set to one hand, which is exactly what Atlassian built a Figma plugin and an ESLint rule to achieve at company scale.

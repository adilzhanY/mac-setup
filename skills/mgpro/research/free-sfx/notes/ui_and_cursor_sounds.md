## Q1. Where do editors get realistic mouse, Magic Mouse, trackpad and keyboard sounds? Specific recordings, packs and licences

### Takeaway
The best free, licence-clean sources are Freesound with the CC0 filter (several real mouse and MacBook trackpad recordings), Kenney's CC0 UI packs (50 and 100 files), Pixabay (free commercial use, no attribution, no standalone resale) and Mixkit (56 free "click" effects under the Mixkit Licence). A real Apple Magic Mouse recording was not found on any free site; only paid libraries (Motion Array, Audio Shade CLICKS) list one.

## Q2. Click anatomy: press and release, duration, attack, brightness, close mic vs room

### Takeaway
A single on screen click is best served by one short, dry, close miked transient (the press), with the release optional and quieter. Sources agree on short (roughly 30 to 150 ms for a tap, under 300 ms for any UI cue), bright (energy around 2 to 8 kHz) and mostly dry, with at most a tiny room reflection for physical button foley. I found no source that measures press to release gaps on real mice.

### Cited Findings
- Taps and clicks should be in the 30 to 150 ms range; most interface cues last under 300 ms, sit in a "pleasant mid-high frequency range" and share a tonal family. (Source: [Violet Recording, How to design UI and app sounds](https://violetrecording.com/how-to-design-ui-sounds/)) (search summary, page not opened, attribution to this exact page is likely but not confirmed)
- UI clicks should carry energy around 2 to 8 kHz; 1 to 3 kHz presence helps clicks, taps and small impacts read; 6 to 10 kHz adds brightness but can exaggerate hiss; cues of 50 to 300 ms cut through small speakers better than long sweeps. (Source: [Cursa, Sound design that reads on small speakers](https://cursa.app/en/page/sound-design-that-reads-on-small-speakers))
- Layering for small speakers: identity (the recognisable click core), presence (a short midrange layer, for example a slightly distorted knock), body (quiet low thud, only helps on good speakers), tail (very short room reflection, subtle). Tap or select: "a short click with a tiny high tick"; confirm: slightly lower click plus a soft chime; error: short buzz with midrange emphasis. (Source: [Cursa](https://cursa.app/en/page/sound-design-that-reads-on-small-speakers))
- A sound library vendor says UI clicks should be audible without feeling sharp, most interface clicks are best kept dry, but a tiny room reflection can help physical button foley feel real in product videos. (Source: [media.io click sound effects](https://www.media.io/audio-effects/click-sound-effects.html)) (search summary, vendor page)
- Remotion's CC0 mouse click file is about 0.397 s long in total, so it likely holds press, release and decay in one file. (Source: [Remotion mouseClick docs](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/sfx/mouse-click))
- Recordings exist that separate press and release: Freesound 687108 "Mouse 2 Button Long Click" (button held down) and OpenGameArt "Middle mouse click" (press and release sounds). (Source: [Freesound CC0 search](https://freesound.org/search/?q=mouse+click&f=license%3A%22Creative+Commons+0%22&s=Downloads+%28most+first%29), [OpenGameArt middle mouse click](https://opengameart.org/content/middle-mouse-click))
- Sync point is the transient: align the sharp attack with the action frame, not the start of the audio file, because files with a lead in will land late. (Source: [Morphic, how to sync sound effects to video](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/how-to-sync-sound-effects-to-video))

### Inferences
- For a single cursor click, use the press transient only, or press plus a release 6 to 10 dB quieter; at 30 fps one frame is 33 ms, so a real 40 to 100 ms press to release gap would put the release 1 to 3 frames later. Placing the release on the frame where the on screen button visually pops back is a natural rule. This is my reasoning from frame arithmetic, not a sourced practice.
- Trim leading silence on every downloaded click so the transient sits at sample 0; this makes frame placement exact (follows from the Morphic transient rule).
- Close miked, dry recordings are preferred because a room tail smears over the next click and over voice-over; add any "space" as a shared, short reverb in the mix instead.

### Gaps
- No measured data found on real mouse press to release intervals (the 40 to 100 ms figure in the brief is unverified).
- No source specifies attack times in ms for UI clicks.

## Q3. Typing sounds for on screen text fields: avoiding the machine gun

### Takeaway
Editors either use a typing sync script (After Effects / Videohive tools that place a sound per character) or hand place a subtle keyboard bed. The anti machine gun tools are multiple single keystroke variants, different velocities, and small random pitch and gain changes; I found no source with exact counts from a video editor.

### Cited Findings
- Videohive "Type Sync Pro" lets you select one or more sounds that it syncs automatically with the typing; Motion Princess "Text & Keyboards" combines text and keyboard for synchronized typing animation in one click. (Source: [Videohive Type Sync Pro](https://videohive.net/item/type-sync-pro-realistic-writing-tool/43443401), [Motion Princess Text & Keyboards](https://motionprincess.com/text-and-keyboards/))
- A Premiere Pro typewriter tutorial advises adding a subtle keyboard sound and aligning it with the typing effect on the timeline. (Source: [FreeVisuals typing effect in Premiere Pro](https://www.freevisuals.net/post/how-to-make-a-typing-effect-in-premiere-pro))
- To avoid fatigue, alternate several variants of each click and slightly vary pitch or gain; a Remotion oriented guide suggests small random playback rate and gain changes. (Source: [skills.sh jeremielim ui-sound-design](https://www.skills.sh/jeremielim/skills/ui-sound-design), [creatorsoundspro on UI sound](https://creatorsoundspro.com/the-power-of-sonic-microinteractions-in-game-ux-a-deep-dive-into-ui-sound-effects)) (search summary, pages not opened, exact page for each claim not confirmed)
- Keep related actions in one sound family and vary pitch or timbre slightly to reduce fatigue. (Source: [Cursa](https://cursa.app/en/page/sound-design-that-reads-on-small-speakers))
- Material for variation: OpenGameArt pack with 10 typing sounds at different velocities and 32 single keypress sounds; shapeforms Type library with single keystrokes from 4 keyboard types. (Source: [OpenGameArt node 122745](https://opengameart.org/node/122745), [shapeforms Type](https://shapeforms.itch.io/type))

### Inferences
- Practical recipe (my synthesis, not a quoted rule): 6 to 10 single keystroke files from one keyboard, never the same file twice in a row, random pitch of about plus or minus 1 to 2 semitones (or 3 to 6 percent playback rate) and gain of plus or minus 2 to 3 dB, a slightly heavier sound for space and enter. On screen text that types faster than a human (common in ads, often 1 character per frame) should not get one sound per character; place sounds on roughly every second or third character, or use a short real typing burst under the whole word, so the rate stays under about 10 to 12 keys per second, which is fast human typing. Typing should sit lower than the hero clicks.

### Gaps
- No editor or forum source found giving specific numbers of variants or pitch ranges for typing in product videos. Reddit threads did not surface in search.

No primary source describes the UI sound approach of Apple, Linear, Notion or Arc product films. Whether to use sounds at all is a style choice I could not document from the brands themselves.

### Inferences
- If the product itself makes no sounds (most web apps), designed soft ticks that are consistent with each other read better than literal desktop mouse clicks, and a real click is best reserved for moments where a cursor visibly presses. This follows from IRPR's "match the product" rule, but it is my interpretation.

### Gaps

## Q5. Levels against music and voice-over; staying audible on phone speakers

### Takeaway
There is no published industry standard for click level relative to music. One tutorial style guide puts primary button clicks about 6 dB above the music bed, with music at about minus 24 to minus 20 LUFS under voice-over; game audio advice to keep UI 10 to 20 dB under everything else does not apply to an ad where clicks carry the story. For phone speakers, the click's identity must live above about 200 Hz, ideally 1 to 8 kHz.

### Cited Findings
- A Remotion focused UI sound guide gives per element offsets against the music: subtle boosts for small control clicks and about +6 dB for primary buttons; music bed around minus 24 to minus 20 LUFS, ducked under voice-over; a table maps on screen actions to sound type and level relative to music. (Source: [skills.sh jeremielim ui-sound-design](https://www.skills.sh/jeremielim/skills/ui-sound-design), [skillsdirectory razee4315 sound-design](https://www.skillsdirectory.com/skills/razee4315-sound-design)) (search summary; which of these pages holds the table is not confirmed; it is a tutorial skill file, one opinion not a standard)
- Contradicting context: a game audio blog recommends keeping UI sounds roughly 10 to 20 dB below other elements so they do not fight music and effects. (Source: [creatorsoundspro on UI sound in games](https://creatorsoundspro.com/the-power-of-sonic-microinteractions-in-game-ux-a-deep-dive-into-ui-sound-effects)) (search summary; game UI context, not ads)
- UI sounds should sit under the voice but remain noticeable; dip music about 1 to 3 dB under dense lines; nudge key effects up slightly on the exact frame of the action rather than raising their overall level. (Source: [Cursa](https://cursa.app/en/page/sound-design-that-reads-on-small-speakers))
- Phone speakers roll off below roughly 150 to 200 Hz, so click identity must not depend on low end; for low sounds, duplicate, high pass at 120 to 200 Hz, add light saturation, boost 1 to 2.5 kHz. (Source: [Cursa](https://cursa.app/en/page/sound-design-that-reads-on-small-speakers))
- Small speaker guides: 2 to 3 kHz is the clarity zone, cut harshness around 3 to 5 kHz if a sound bites; phones act effectively mono; check the mix on a real device. (Source: [Waves, 8 tips for small speakers](https://www.waves.com/tips-for-great-mixes-on-small-speakers), [Sound On Sound, mixing for mobile](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/mixing-mobile?amp)) (search summary; sources disagree on exact bands)
- Masters must be loudness compliant for the delivery platform. (Source: [IRPR app videos](https://sounddesign.irpr.agency/applications/app-videos/))

### Inferences
- For an ad where the click drives the story: peak level of a hero click roughly 4 to 8 dB above the momentary music level at that instant, and about level with or a few dB under the voice-over peaks when they overlap (better: do not overlap a hero click with a stressed word). Duck the music 1 to 3 dB for 100 to 200 ms around the hero click if it still hides. Secondary clicks and typing 4 to 8 dB under the hero click. These numbers combine the +6 dB guide and the Cursa "nudge on the frame" rule; they are a starting point, not a measured standard.
- High pass every click at about 150 to 200 Hz and check that it still reads; if it vanishes, it was mostly thump and will vanish on a phone.
- Keep clicks centred (mono) since phones are effectively mono.

### Gaps
- No mixer or editor quote found with a specific "clicks X dB above music" number for product ads. Reddit and motion design forum threads did not surface in searches.

## Q6. Timing relative to the visual press (frame offsets)

### Takeaway
Put the click transient on the frame where the button visibly depresses (or the cursor lands its press state). Broadcast research shows audio leading picture is noticed sooner (about 45 ms) than audio lagging (about 125 ms), so a click must never be early by more than one frame; up to one frame late is safe. Netflix QC treats one frame or less as acceptable and two frames or more as an issue.

### Cited Findings
- ITU-R BT.1359-1: detectability thresholds about +45 ms (sound ahead of picture) to minus 125 ms (sound behind); acceptability about +90 ms to minus 185 ms. (Source: [ITU-R BT.1359-1 PDF](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bt/R-REC-BT.1359-1-199811-I!!PDF-E.pdf), [Wikipedia, audio-to-video synchronization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio-to-video_synchronization)); a USPTO patent gives the acceptability lag figure as minus 190 ms instead of minus 185 ms. (Source: [USPTO patent 7400653](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/7400653))
- Netflix sync QC: effects 1 frame or less early or late are logged FYI; 2 or more frames early or late are logged as an Issue. (Source: [Netflix Backlot, Sync Effects](https://backlothelp.netflix.com/hc/en-us/articles/115000533952-Sync-Effects))
- Align the transient, not the file start. (Source: [Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/how-to-sync-sound-effects-to-video))

### Inferences
- At 30 fps one frame is 33 ms and at 60 fps 17 ms; 45 ms lead is just over one frame at 30 fps. So: transient exactly on the press frame, or 0 to 1 frame after it; never 2 frames before. If the animation has a squash (button shrinks over 2 to 3 frames), put the click where the squash reaches its smallest size.

### Gaps
- No source found confirming the editor folk rule "put the sound a frame early"; the ITU asymmetry argues against leading.

## Q7. Overused UI sounds to avoid; repetition

### Takeaway
I found no source listing the iPhone keyboard click, Windows click or Mac "Tink" as clichés by name. Sources do say that putting a pop on every text overlay and a whoosh on every move makes the sounds meaningless, and that unmodified stock effects make visuals feel cheap.

### Cited Findings
- When every camera move gets a whoosh and every text overlay gets a pop, the sounds lose meaning; adding effects to every cut, zoom or text pop quickly annoys viewers; fewer, purposeful effects work better. (Source: [SFX Engine, common sound design mistakes 2026](https://sfxengine.com/blog/common-sound-design-mistakes-in-video-editing), [SFX Engine, video editing sound effects](https://sfxengine.com/blog/video-editing-sound-effects)) (search summary)
- Relying on unmodified stock sound effects can make high budget visuals feel cheap; customise or layer them and match the sound's character to the visual style (for example heavy metallic clanks on clean digital text pop ups do not match). (Source: [SFX Engine](https://sfxengine.com/blog/common-sound-design-mistakes-in-video-editing))

### Inferences
- OS system sounds (macOS Tink, Pop, Funk; Windows Navigation Start click; the iPhone keyboard tick) are also Apple and Microsoft property bundled with their OS, so beyond being recognisable they are not licensed for reuse in ads. I did not verify the licence terms; treat this as a reason to avoid them rather than a confirmed legal finding.
- Use at most one sound per meaningful action; let micro motions (hover, small fades) stay silent.

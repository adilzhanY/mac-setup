# Sound timing, rhythm, silence and mixing against picture in motion graphics and short commercials

Frame conversions used throughout: 1 frame = 33.3 ms at 30 fps, 16.7 ms at 60 fps (41.7 ms at 24 fps). Signed offsets follow the broadcast convention: "+" means audio leads (arrives before) the picture, "-" means audio lags.

## 1. Hit points: on the frame, before it, or after? Offsets for whooshes, impacts, UI taps, risers; J and L cuts

### Takeaway
Impacts and taps go on the frame of contact (or at most 1 frame early, never late by more than 1 to 2 frames); whooshes and risers are placed before the cut so their peak or their abrupt stop lands on the first frame of the new shot. The human detectability window is asymmetric: audio leading by more than about 45 ms (1.35 frames at 30 fps, 2.7 at 60 fps) is noticed sooner than audio lagging (about 125 ms), so the safe zone for "hard sync" is 0 to 1 frame early, and long pre-roll belongs only to sounds whose job is anticipation.

### Cited Findings
- ITU-R BT.1359-1 (1998): detectability threshold for A/V offset is about +45 ms (audio leading) to -125 ms (audio lagging); acceptability limit about +90 ms to -185 ms (one source reports -190 ms). At 30 fps: +1.35 / -3.75 frames detectable; +2.7 / -5.6 frames acceptable. At 60 fps: +2.7 / -7.5 frames detectable. The recommendation dates from 1998; one 2026 engineering article says it is still in force (not verified against ITU directly). Source: [Wikipedia: Audio-to-video synchronization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio-to-video_synchronization); [TV Technology: AV synchronization, how bad is bad](https://www.tvtechnology.com/opinions/av-synchronization-how-bad-is-bad); [NAB TV Tech Check 2009](https://nab.org/xert/scitech/2009/TVTechCheck/TV100509.asp); [Fora Soft: lip sync and BT.1359 tolerance windows](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lip-sync-itu-r-bt-1359-tolerance-windows)
- EBU R37: end to end A/V sync should stay within +40 ms (audio early) to -60 ms (audio late), described as the "undetectable plateau" (that is +1.2 / -1.8 frames at 30 fps, +2.4 / -3.6 frames at 60 fps). One glossary describes R37 differently (as tighter production guidance), so sources disagree on its exact framing. Source: [Wikipedia: Audio-to-video synchronization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio-to-video_synchronization); [Fora Soft lip sync glossary](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/glossary/terms-audio/lip-sync)
- Netflix delivery QC (as quoted in search snippets; the page now redirects to a login-walled Knowledge Hub I could not read): sync effects 1 frame or less early or late are "minor"; 2 or more frames off is an issue to fix. It also distinguishes a single effect being off from a consistent offset across a section. Source: [Netflix Partner Help: Sync Effects (redirected)](https://backlothelp.netflix.com/hc/en-us/articles/115000533952-Sync-Effects)
- Practitioners on Gearspace: "sound-early is always worse than sound-late" because early never occurs in nature; another poster says non-professionals start to notice at 1 frame early or 2 frames late. Source: [Gearspace: sync issues](https://gearspace.com/threads/sync-issues.1192176/)
- Contradicting nuance from a vendor guide: "a hair early often reads better than late" because real impacts reach eye and ear almost simultaneously; if a cue "on the frame" still feels late, the visual action often peaks 1 to 2 frames after contact, or the sample has a slow attack, so nudge earlier 1 frame at a time or trim the lead-in. Source: [Morphic: How to sync sound effects to video](https://morphic.com/resources/how-to/how-to-sync-sound-effects-to-video)
- Impacts belong on the cut, not near it: "even 50 ms early reads as sloppy" (50 ms = 1.5 frames at 30 fps, 3 frames at 60 fps). Whoosh length should match the move: for a ~0.5 s transition use a whoosh of "roughly 400-500 ms" (12 to 15 frames at 30 fps; 24 to 30 at 60 fps) that starts just before the frames change and tails off just after. A riser sits about "the second ahead of your key claim". Source: [Zella: whoosh, impact and riser at cuts](https://zellahq.com/blog/sound-design-at-cuts-whoosh-impact-riser/)
- Whoosh placement: start before the edit and land its peak on the first frame of the new shot; a transition sound placed entirely after the cut "always feels late". Suggested lengths 1.5 to 3 s for a cut, 4 to 6 s when building anticipation (single vendor, marketing page). Source: [Morphic: Transition sound effects](https://morphic.com/resources/sounds/transition-sound-effects)
- Reverse whooshes and risers are placed before the moment, not on it: they start quiet, swell, then stop dead where the moment lands; the edit lands in the silence right after, which "hits harder" than a sound that continues through the cut. A short whoosh or riser in the half second before the cut (15 frames at 30 fps, 30 at 60 fps) pulls the ear toward the moment. Source: [add.app: Sound design for trailers](https://add.app/sound-effects/sound-design-for-trailers-hits-rises-drones-pulses/); [Morphic: Whoosh sound effect](https://morphic.com/resources/sounds/whoosh-sound-effect); [Violet Recording: trailer sound design](https://violetrecording.com/sound-design-for-trailers/)
- Align the strongest part of a sound with the exact frame where an animation finishes or changes direction; build sounds around acceleration, contact and settling, not one generic effect per transition. Source: [FEVR: Sound design for motion graphics](https://wearefevr.com/sound-design-for-motion-graphics); [Pippit: sound design for motion graphics](https://www.pippit.ai/blog/sound-design-for-motion-graphics)
- Trailer practice (Derek Lieu, game trailer editor, 2022): audio often starts before the previous scene ends to foreshadow the next one (example: helicopter sound bleeding into the prior scene in The Silence of the Lambs); hits and booms land on sudden definitive motion such as title card slams, edits and transitions; whooshes go with dips to black or white, dissolves and lens flares. No frame numbers given. Source: [Derek Lieu: Secrets to trailer sound design](https://www.derek-lieu.com/blog/2022/1/17/secrets-to-trailer-sound-design)
- Larry Jordan: in motion graphics the audio track usually locks timing before picture is animated ("create the audio first"); SFX added afterward are the exception. Source: [Larry Jordan: Create the audio first for a motion graphic video](https://larryjordan.com/articles/create-the-audio-first-for-a-motion-graphic-video/)
- J cut: next shot's audio starts before the picture changes; L cut: previous shot's audio continues after the picture changes; collectively "split edits". A prelap is a sound or line heard before the cut to the scene, related to the J cut and sound bridge. J cuts tend to lead into a new scene or location, L cuts keep a moment grounded. Source: [Wikipedia: J cut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J_cut); [Wikipedia: Prelap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prelap); [Filmdaft: The L-cut and J-cut](https://filmdaft.com/the-l-cut-and-j-cut-how-film-editors-use-audio-to-control-time/); [Epidemic Sound: J-cuts and L-cuts](https://www.epidemicsound.com/blog/j-cuts-and-l-cuts/)

### Inferences
- Practical offset table derived from the standards above (not a published table): impacts, UI taps, clicks, logo hits: transient on the contact frame, 0 to 1 frame early (0 to 33 ms at 30 fps, 0 to 2 frames / 0 to 33 ms at 60 fps). Never more than about 45 ms early (detectable) and never more than 1 to 2 frames late (Netflix QC). Whooshes: peak on the first frame of the new shot (or the frame of fastest motion), body starting 6 to 15 frames earlier at 30 fps. Risers and reverse whooshes: end abruptly on the cut frame, length 0.5 to 2 s before it.
- In motion design a J cut means letting the next scene's ambience, music change or first SFX enter a few frames before the visual transition; an L cut means letting a hit's reverb tail or the previous section's pad ring through the new scene so the cut does not feel chopped.
- At 60 fps, a "1 frame early" nudge is only 16.7 ms, so 60 fps work allows finer placement; offsets should be thought of in ms and converted per frame rate.

### Gaps
- No primary source (Murch, Sound On Sound, a named commercial mixer) gives explicit "N frames early" numbers for whoosh peaks or UI taps; the numbers above come from standards (detectability) and vendor or practitioner guides.
- Could not read the current Netflix QC page (login-walled redirect); the 1 frame / 2 frame figures come from search snippets.
- No r/audiopost or r/MotionDesign threads surfaced in searches.

## 2. Rhythm: cutting on or against the beat, downbeats, syncopation, groove, tempo by mood, hit density

### Takeaway
Editorial theory ranks rhythm third behind emotion and story (Murch: 10% of a cut's value), so locking every cut to the grid is not the goal. Evidence on tempo in ads is real but narrow: fast music (above about 94 BPM in the classic Milliman definition) raises purchase intent for promotion-focused ads, while slow music can improve recall and affect. Density guidance is qualitative: do not sound every cut.

### Cited Findings
- Murch's Rule of Six (In the Blink of an Eye): Emotion 51%, Story 23%, Rhythm 10%, Eye-trace 7%, 2D screen plane 5%, 3D space 4%; higher items obscure problems with lower ones, not vice versa, and emotion should never be sacrificed for the lower criteria. Source: [No Film School: Murch's 6 rules](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch); [UT Austin RTF wiki: Murch's Rule of Six](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/rtf318/pages/80022510/Murch+s+Rule+of+Six); [Filmdaft: Rule of Six](https://filmdaft.com/walter-murchs-rule-of-six-the-editors-formula-for-choosing-the-right-cut/)
- Journal of Advertising Research study of 26,025 video ads plus three experiments: fast tempo music significantly increases purchase intentions and willingness to pay for promotion-focused ads, with no significant effect for prevention-focused ads; mechanism is time pressure and heuristic processing, not arousal; effect appears only once music is "sufficiently fast". Source: [ARF / JAR: When fast music makes ads more persuasive](https://thearf.org/access-knowledge-2/publications/journal-of-advertising-research/when-fast-music-makes-ads-more-persuasive-and-when-it-doesnt/)
- A restaurant-ad study (Borges 2021) found the opposite mechanism: fast music raised self-reported arousal, then mood, taste expectations and purchase intent. Source: [IDEAS/RePEc: Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services v63, 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joreco/v63y2021ics0969698921002964.html)
- A thesis (Oakes) found slow tempo music produced higher ad recall and more positive affect than fast tempo. Source: [University of Leicester thesis: Cognitive and affective responses to music in ads](https://figshare.le.ac.uk/articles/thesis/Cognitive_and_affective_responses_to_music_in_ads_and_service_environments/10153406)
- Common academic cutoff: tempo above 94 BPM is "fast" (Milliman 1982, cited secondhand). Source: [Immerse Education: Effects of fast tempo background music (PDF)](https://www.immerse.education/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Effects-of-fast-tempo-background-music.pdf)
- Density: do not put a sound on every cut; sound section changes, punch-ins and reveals. In a video cutting every 4 to 5 s, sounding every join makes the mix sound like a percussion track. Source: [Zella](https://zellahq.com/blog/sound-design-at-cuts-whoosh-impact-riser/)
- Common motion graphics mistakes: out of sync audio, generic effects with no rhythm, music that ignores the visual tempo, cluttered busy sequences. Land action on music beats where possible. Source: [IRPR Sound: motion graphics](https://sounddesign.irpr.agency/applications/motion-graphics/); [Pippit](https://www.pippit.ai/blog/sound-design-for-motion-graphics)
- Trailers can carry "well over a dozen" SFX tracks, but the skill "doesn't come from the quantity of sound"; each sound must serve a purpose. Source: [Derek Lieu](https://www.derek-lieu.com/blog/2022/1/17/secrets-to-trailer-sound-design)

### Inferences
- Beat math for planning (arithmetic, not sourced): one beat = 60/BPM s. At 120 BPM a beat is 500 ms = 15 frames at 30 fps (30 at 60 fps), a bar of 4/4 is 2 s = 60 frames. At 100 BPM a beat is 18 frames at 30 fps; at 90 BPM, 20 frames; at 128 BPM, 14.06 frames (not an integer, so cuts drift unless snapped to the nearest frame). Tempos that give whole frames per beat at 30 fps: 60, 72, 75, 90, 100, 112.5, 120, 128.57, 150, 180 BPM. At 60 fps there are twice as many whole-frame options (for example 120 BPM = 30 frames, 128 BPM = 28.1 frames, still non-integer).
- A reasonable working pattern consistent with Murch: put structural changes (scene changes, logo, CTA) on downbeats or bar starts, let smaller motions fall on off-beats or between beats so the piece does not feel mechanical.
- Given the "do not sound every cut" advice, one accented SFX per 1 to 2 s of busy section and fewer in calm sections is a plausible ceiling, but this is an inference, not a measured figure.

### Gaps
- No credible source gave BPM ranges by mood specifically for ads (for example "tech launch 110 to 128 BPM"); only the 94 BPM fast/slow split and the JAR findings are sourced.
- No source quantified hit density per second (rich versus cluttered), percentage of edits on downbeats, swing amount, or groove versus quantized grid in commercials.
- Murch's book itself was not accessed; Rule of Six figures are via secondary sources that agree with each other.

## 3. Silence and dynamics over a 15 to 60 second film

### Takeaway
Near silence immediately before the biggest hit is the standard trailer device for making that hit feel huge, and a riser that stops dead lets the cut land in silence. For short form, broadcast specs themselves cap how loud any moment can be (EBU R128 s1: short-term loudness never above -18 LUFS on a -23 LUFS ad), so contrast must come from going quieter, not louder.

### Cited Findings
- A braam is "only huge because of the beat of near-silence right before it"; "one of the most powerful trailer techniques is the sudden cut to near-silence right before the final hit." Source: [add.app: Sound design for trailers](https://add.app/sound-effects/sound-design-for-trailers-hits-rises-drones-pulses/); [Tonal Chaos: cinematic sound design for trailers](https://tonalchaostrailers.com/blog/sound-design-for-film-trailers/)
- A hit suits a new shot that needs weight; silence suits one that needs air; a swell that cuts out at its loudest can make the next scene feel more deliberate than another impact. Source: [Morphic: Transition sound effects](https://morphic.com/resources/sounds/transition-sound-effects)
- Longer risers can act almost like music and build to a climax at the end of a trailer; trailers often open on a low boom. Source: [Derek Lieu](https://www.derek-lieu.com/blog/2022/1/17/secrets-to-trailer-sound-design)
- EBU R128 s1 (2020) for adverts and promos: programme loudness -23.0 LUFS (+-0.2 LU QC tolerance), maximum short-term loudness (3 s window) -18.0 LUFS (+5 LU relative), max true peak -1 dBTP; loudness range is not used for short-form because a 30 s spot has too few 3 s data points. Content may be deliberately lower than -23 if flagged. Source: [EBU R 128 s1 (2020) PDF](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128s1.pdf)
- For cinema ad playback, a forum thread advises measuring with Dolby tools at 82 dB Leq(m), not compressing too much and keeping the mix dynamic. Source: [Avid DUC: First ad mix for cinema](https://duc.avid.com/archive/index.php/t-280573.html)
- Over-compressing a commercial mix can make the voice feel quieter than less compressed spots once everything is loudness normalized; one mixer uses only a couple of dB of multiband and limiting on the bus. Source: [Gearspace: Mix bus processing TV commercials](https://gearspace.com/board/post-production-forum/1276450-mix-bus-processing-tv-commercials.html)

### Inferences
- Because every platform normalizes integrated loudness, a dynamic mix (quiet verses, loud payoff) is effectively free: the quiet parts do not make the piece "quieter" overall, they make the payoff relatively bigger. On EBU R128 s1 the payoff can be at most 5 LU above the average.
- A typical shape implied by trailer practice: establish (music and SFX), build (riser, density up), gap of near silence of roughly 0.25 to 1 s before the reveal, payoff hit on the logo or product reveal, then a short tail for the CTA. The silence length range is an inference; no source gave numbers.
- The loudest moment is normally the product or logo reveal near the end (last 20 to 30% of the film) per the trailer "climax at the end" pattern; no study quantifies this for 15 to 60 s ads.

### Gaps
- No source gave exact silence durations (ms) before reveals, or measured where the loudest moment sits in award-winning short commercials.
- No interviews found with Lime Studios, Barking Owl, Sonic Union, Wave Studios, Factory or String and Tins on dynamics.

## 4. Music vs SFX vs voice-over balance: levels, ducking, EQ carving, frequency slots, stereo, reverb

### Takeaway
Set the VO first, then fit music roughly 18 to 20 dB under speech and SFX roughly 6 to 18 dB under it depending on how featured they are. Ducking starts at about 6 to 12 dB of gain reduction with a 10 to 80 ms attack and 200 to 700 ms release; over-ducking (15 to 20 dB on every line) is the most common mistake. Carve music in the 1 to 4 kHz intelligibility band with small (1 to 3 dB) moves rather than big notches.

### Cited Findings
- Starting balance: music about -18 to -20 dB below speech, SFX about -12 to -18 dB below speech; no universal setting. Source: [Pure Audio Insight: How loud should background music be](https://pureaudioinsight.com/blogs/content-production/background-music-volume-how-loud-should-it-be)
- Alternative SFX figure: "around -6 dB relative to speech is a good default" for cut sounds (featured transitions, not ambience). Source: [Zella](https://zellahq.com/blog/sound-design-at-cuts-whoosh-impact-riser/)
- A rough percentage guide (single source): background music 40 to 60% volume, SFX 60 to 80%. Low reliability. Source: [Pippit](https://www.pippit.ai/blog/sound-design-for-motion-graphics)
- Broadcast practice: one forum mixer sets VO to the broadcast spec first (-24 LUFS, Australian OP-59) and fits music and effects around it; collisions are handled mostly by fader automation, with only a couple of dB of voice-keyed ducking on full mixes. Source: [Gearspace: Mix bus processing TV commercials](https://gearspace.com/board/post-production-forum/1276450-mix-bus-processing-tv-commercials.html)
- Ducking ranges compiled from editing guides: depth 6 to 10 dB to start (gentle dip 6 to 12 dB; some vendors call -20 dB typical); attack 30 to 80 ms; release 300 to 500 ms, lengthen to 400 to 700 ms if music pumps between phrases; over-ducking by 15 to 20 dB on every line is the most common mistake. Example defaults: one plugin -20 dB threshold, 10 ms attack, 500 ms release, 200 ms hold; another -30 dBFS threshold, 4:1 ratio, 10 ms attack, 200 ms release, -20 dB max reduction. Source: [Vindral Composer: Sidechain ducking](https://composer-docs.vindral.com/operators/operators-sidechain-ducking.html); [United Plugins VoxDucker docs](https://develop.unitedplugins.com/VoxDucker/documentation); [CapCut: audio ducking](https://www.capcut.com/create/audio-ducking-for-clear-dialogue-in-video); [DaVinci Resolve Club: audio ducking](https://davinciresolveclub.com/davinci-resolve-audio-ducking.md)
- EQ carving: boost voice and cut music in 1200 to 2400 Hz ("critical for consonants"); Fairlight guidance: 600 Hz to 1.1 kHz is nasal or honky (cut narrowly, only if confirmed), 2 to 5 kHz is intelligibility and presence (broad small boost for clarity, cut for harshness); small 1 to 3 dB moves sound more natural; a 2 to 4 kHz dip dulls presence. A forum tip: pop the voice's hi mids and compress about 4:1, with automated EQ on the music. iZotope Neutron instances can communicate to cut buildup on other tracks. Source: [DV Info Net: music and dialogue, punching a hole](https://www.dvinfo.net/forum/archive/index.php/t-41476.html); [DaVinci Resolve Club: Fairlight dialogue EQ](https://davinciresolveclub.com/fairlight-eq-dialogue.md); [Production Expert: Should you carve EQ when mixing](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/should-you-to-carve-eq-when-mixing); [Ripple Training: EQ carving in Final Cut Pro X](https://www.rippletraining.com/?p=17943)
- Commercial context: "there's probably more audio processing in a national TV commercial than in a top-10 music hit or a feature film." Source: [Gearspace](https://gearspace.com/board/post-production-forum/1276450-mix-bus-processing-tv-commercials.html)
- Trailers: existing sounds are sweetened (made more clicky, boomy, aggressive) and a final pro mixer balances them into a cohesive whole. Source: [Derek Lieu](https://www.derek-lieu.com/blog/2022/1/17/secrets-to-trailer-sound-design)

### Inferences
- Frequency slots (general mixing convention, not sourced here): music bed owns roughly 60 to 250 Hz and the pads in 300 Hz to 1 kHz; VO owns 1 to 5 kHz; UI ticks and clicks live above 2 to 3 kHz (they cut through without volume); impacts take the sub (30 to 80 Hz) for a few frames, so sidechaining the music bass to the impact for 100 to 300 ms keeps the hit clean. Whooshes are mostly broadband noise and need a 1 to 3 dB dip in the music around the peak, or simple fader automation, which matches the broadcast mixer's "automate rather than duck" habit.
- Without VO (typical for product motion graphics), the same sidechain logic can key the music off the SFX bus with lighter settings (2 to 4 dB, fast attack 1 to 10 ms, release 100 to 250 ms) so hits punch through; this is an extrapolation from the VO ducking numbers.
- Stereo: keep VO and key impacts in the centre (they must survive mono phone speakers); place whooshes panned along on-screen motion direction, with the pan resolving to centre at the hit frame. Reverb: send SFX to the same short room or plate as the music (or a reverb tuned to the music's) to glue them; keep VO dry. These are common practice, unsourced in this research.

### Gaps
- No sourced figures for stereo placement or reverb send levels in commercial or motion graphics mixes.
- No interviews or articles from Lime, Barking Owl, Sonic Union, Wave, Factory or String and Tins surfaced with numeric balance or ducking settings.
- The 40 to 60% and -6 dB SFX figures are single-source rules of thumb and conflict with the -12 to -18 dB figure; they apply to different SFX roles.

## 5. Mastering and loudness targets (2026)

### Takeaway
Broadcast is the only area with hard, published specs: EBU R128 -23 LUFS, -1 dBTP (ads: short-term max -18 LUFS); ATSC A/85 -24 LKFS, -2 dBTP (CALM Act); Netflix -27 LKFS dialog-gated, -2 dBTP. For web and social, YouTube's -14 LUFS (turn-down only) is the de facto reference; TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn and X publish no target, so -14 LUFS integrated, -1 dBTP is the defensible default, with many creators mastering hotter (-10 to -12 LUFS) for phone feeds.

### Cited Findings
- EBU R128 s1 (August 2020, short-form): -23.0 LUFS +-0.2 LU, max short-term -18.0 LUFS, max true peak -1 dBTP, no LRA requirement for short-form. Source: [EBU R 128 s1 (2020)](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128s1.pdf)
- EBU R128 general: -23 LUFS (+-0.5 LU in body text; glossary says +-1 LU, an internal inconsistency), -1 dBTP. ATSC A/85: -24 LKFS, -2 dBTP recommended, mandatory for US TV ads under the CALM Act. Netflix: -27 LKFS dialog-gated (+-2 LU), -2 dBTP, falls back to about -24 LKFS full programme if dialogue is insufficient. Source: [Fora Soft: LUFS targets per platform 2026](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lufs-targets-per-platform-2026)
- YouTube: -14 LUFS, -1 dBTP (cross-checked against "Stats for nerds"); YouTube and Apple Sound Check largely only turn content down, so quiet masters stay quiet; Spotify, TIDAL, Amazon, Deezer also turn up. Example: a -10 LUFS upload is lowered 4 dB on YouTube. Source: [Fora Soft 2026](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lufs-targets-per-platform-2026); [Wikipedia: Audio normalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio_normalization)
- Spotify Normal -14 LUFS (Loud -11, Quiet -19), -1 dBTP (-2 recommended on upload); Apple Music -16 LUFS, -1 dBTP; Apple Podcasts -16 +-1; Deezer -15; TIDAL/Amazon/SoundCloud -14. AES TD1008 streaming guidance: music -16, speech -18, mixed -17 LUFS, -1 dBTP. Source: [Fora Soft 2026](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lufs-targets-per-platform-2026)
- TikTok, Instagram, Meta: no published target (about -14 internal, context adaptive); Meta uses adaptive loudness over xHE-AAC; -1 dBTP advised; do not put a fixed number in a contract. Creators often master hot (-10 to -12 LUFS) for phone feeds, which is a content strategy, not a standard. Source: [Fora Soft 2026](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lufs-targets-per-platform-2026)
- Conflicting vendor claims: one guide says Instagram and TikTok prefer -12 to -10 LUFS and Facebook -13 LUFS (no official source); another says -14 to -16 is a safe range for TikTok and Reels. Source: [Opus: Best loudness normalizers](https://opus.pro/blog/best-loudness-normalizers); [OpenClip: LUFS targets by platform](https://openclip.app/learn/audio-normalization)
- Apple App Store app previews (official): stereo, 256 kbps AAC (H.264) or PCM/AAC (ProRes 422 HQ), 44.1 or 48 kHz, all tracks enabled; 15 to 30 s; max 30 fps; max 500 MB; H.264 at 10 to 12 Mbps. No loudness target is published, and the page does not state whether an audio track is mandatory (third-party guides conflict: some say a silent stereo track is required, Gummicube says audio is optional). Source: [Apple App Store Connect Help: App preview specifications](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-information/app-preview-specifications); [Gummicube: App Store app preview video](https://www.gummicube.com/blog/app-store-app-preview-video/); [DEV: App Store compliant previews with ffmpeg](https://dev.to/picoable/creating-app-store-compliant-app-previews-with-ffmpeg-54m8)
- Level versus loudness: two commercials can both peak at -10 dBFS and sound totally different in loudness; delivery peak specs vary by channel (one mixer delivered from -12 up to 0 dBFS). Confirm specs with broadcaster or agency. Source: [Gearspace](https://gearspace.com/board/post-production-forum/1276450-mix-bus-processing-tv-commercials.html)
- Cinema ads: about 82 dB Leq(m) via Dolby metering. Source: [Avid DUC](https://duc.avid.com/archive/index.php/t-280573.html)

### Inferences
- Google Play's preview is a YouTube link, so YouTube's -14 LUFS normalization applies (inference from Play's YouTube-hosted preview model; not verified in this research).
- Since YouTube only turns down, a -14 LUFS, -1 dBTP master is the safe single deliverable for web, YouTube, Play, app store and social; a separate -23 LUFS / -18 short-term max / -1 dBTP (EU) or -24 LKFS / -2 dBTP (US) version is needed for broadcast. A website autoplay video starts muted, so its loudness matters only after the user unmutes; matching -14 to -16 LUFS avoids blasting.
- Use -1 dBTP minimum (or -2 dBTP) for anything that will be lossy encoded (AAC, Opus, xHE-AAC), because encoding can raise inter-sample peaks.

### Gaps
- No official loudness documentation found for YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn or X. YouTube's -14 is from measurement, not a published spec. LinkedIn and X figures: none found.
- Apple app preview loudness: none published.
- The ATSC A/85 and Netflix numbers come from a 2026 vendor summary, not the primary PDFs (both are well established, but not re-verified here).

## 6. Small speakers, low end, and muted autoplay checks

### Takeaway
Phone speakers cannot reproduce the fundamental of bass and kicks, so pros add harmonics (saturation or harmonic synthesis like Waves MaxxBass) that the brain uses to infer the missing fundamental, extending perceived bass about two octaves below the speaker's limit. For muted autoplay (2019-era data: 69% of people watch with sound off in public, 85% of Facebook video watched without sound), the film must work on captions and visual rhythm alone.

### Cited Findings
- Missing fundamental: small loudspeakers may not produce low frequencies (example: a missing 100 Hz component), yet the pitch of the fundamental is still perceived from its harmonics. Source: [Wikipedia: Missing fundamental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missing_fundamental)
- Production Expert (Luke Goddard, 21 Nov 2024): small speakers are "simply too small to resolve low frequencies at any kind of useful level"; harmonic synthesis can extend perceived response "about two octaves below its physical limitation"; tools named: Waves MaxxBass (splits at a crossover, generates harmonics from content below it) and FabFilter Saturn 2 multiband saturation so the bottom end is preserved while adding harmonics. No crossover frequency given. Source: [Production Expert: Make sure bass is heard on small speaker devices](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/how-to-make-sure-bass-is-heard-on-small-speaker-devices)
- Method: duplicate the bass, saturate, high-pass the copy, blend it back; add only a few harmonics, do not overdo it; check on speakers and headphones too. A forum reply (anecdotal) puts audible "low end" on phone speakers at about 110 to 800 Hz. Source: [FaderPro: Mixing bass for all speaker sizes](https://blog.faderpro.com/mixing/mixing-bass-for-all-speaker-sizes/); [Reddit r/trapproduction thread (mirror)](https://reddit.birdcat.cafe/r/trapproduction/comments/1ktsigc/808low_end_mixing); [Master Your Mix: low end on smaller speakers](https://masteryourmix.com/?p=4342)
- Phone and earbud listening in noisy places favours a denser, more consistent signal. Source: [Fora Soft 2026](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/audio-for-video/articles-audio/lufs-targets-per-platform-2026)
- Muted viewing: Verizon Media and Publicis Media (2019): 69% view video with sound off in public, 25% in private; 80% more likely to finish a video if captions are available. Facebook "85% watched without sound" traces to a 2016/2019 Digiday report of internal data. Newer 2026 blog figures (74% Facebook sound-off attributed to Clipchamp; 92% attributed to Verizon/Publicis) conflict and look unreliable. PLY Media: 66% completion without captions versus 91% with. All are secondhand. Source: [Rev: Top 7 places you watch videos on mute](https://www.rev.com/blog/speech-to-text-accessibility/top-7-places-you-need-to-watch-videos-on-mute); [Hypebot: 85% watch your videos with the sound off](https://www.hypebot.com/85-watch-your-videos-with-the-sound-off-plus-what-you-can-do-about-it.md); [Beey: Captions increase viewership](https://www.beey.io/en/captions-increase-viewership-accessibility-and-reach-23486/); [Mean CEO: Silent video viewing statistics 2026](https://blog.mean.ceo/?p=11554)

### Inferences
- Small speaker check routine (common practice, unsourced here): mono fold-down check (phone speakers are often effectively mono, so anything panned or phase-wide can vanish); high-pass the monitor at about 150 to 200 Hz to simulate a phone and confirm kicks, impacts and bass are still felt through their harmonics (upper bass 150 to 800 Hz and click transients 2 to 5 kHz); listen on an actual phone and laptop at low volume; confirm VO intelligibility under music on the phone.
- Impacts and logo hits should have a mid-range or high "click" layer (2 to 5 kHz) and a 100 to 300 Hz body layer, not only a sub layer below 60 Hz, or they disappear on phones.
- Muted autoplay check: watch the cut with audio off. Every beat that the music carries (rhythm, reveal timing) must also be carried by motion: cut on the motion accents that would land on the beat, keep text on screen long enough to read, and burn in captions for any VO. Since motion graphics are usually animated to a locked audio track (Larry Jordan), the visual rhythm usually survives muting if hits were placed on motion events rather than only in the audio.

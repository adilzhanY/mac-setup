# Books and papers

## Expertise

- Chi, M. T. H., Feltovich, P. J., Glaser, R. (1981). Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices. Cognitive Science 5(2), 121-152. https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA100301.pdf (tech report). Gives the skill: experts sort problems by deep principle, novices by surface features, so name the deep type first.
- Chi, M. T. H., Glaser, R., Farr, M. J. (eds.) (1988). The Nature of Expertise. Erlbaum. Gives the skill: the seven point list of expert traits, including long qualitative analysis before solving.
- Larkin, J., McDermott, J., Simon, D. P., Simon, H. A. (1980). Expert and novice performance in solving physics problems. Science 208(4450), 1335-1342. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.208.4450.1335. Gives the skill: experts reason forward from the givens, novices hunt backward from the unknown.
- Chase, W. G., Simon, H. A. (1973). Perception in chess. Cognitive Psychology 4(1), 55-81. https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(73)90004-2. Gives the skill: expertise is meaningful patterns learned from real cases, useless on random input.
- Ericsson, K. A., Krampe, R. T., Tesch-Romer, C. (1993). The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance. Psychological Review 100(3), 363-406. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731745/ (replication and review). Gives the skill: expertise comes from feedback driven practice on weaknesses.
- Ericsson, K. A., Pool, R. (2016). Peak: Secrets from the New Science of Expertise. Houghton Mifflin Harcourt. Gives the skill: mental representations are what an agent must borrow from the expert.
- Macnamara, B. N., Hambrick, D. Z., Oswald, F. L. (2014). Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: a meta-analysis. Psychological Science 25(8), 1608-1618. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535810. Gives the skill: years of practice predict little in professions, so years alone are a weak expert credential.
- Klein, G. A., Calderwood, R., Clinton-Cirocco, A. (1986). Rapid decision making on the fire ground. Proceedings of the Human Factors Society 30, 576-580. https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA199492.pdf. Gives the skill: experts recognize the situation type, then simulate one action, rarely comparing options.
- Klein, G. (1998). Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions. MIT Press. Gives the skill: experts spend their effort on situation assessment and notice missing cues.
- Klein, G. A., Calderwood, R., MacGregor, D. (1989). Critical decision method for eliciting knowledge. IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics 19(3), 462-472. https://doi.org/10.1109/21.31053. Gives the skill: the probe questions to put to the imagined expert (cues, typicality, analogues, what ifs).
- Klein, G. (2007). Performing a project premortem. Harvard Business Review 85(9), 18-19. https://hbr.org/2007/09/performing-a-project-premortem. Gives the skill: assume it failed, list why, turn the causes into moves.
- Mitchell, D. J., Russo, J. E., Pennington, N. (1989). Back to the future: temporal perspective in the explanation of events. Journal of Behavioral Decision Making 2(1), 25-38. https://doi.org/10.1002/bdm.3960020103. Gives the skill: the research basis for prospective hindsight behind the pre-mortem.
- Dreyfus, S. E., Dreyfus, H. L. (1980). A five-stage model of the mental activities involved in directed skill acquisition. ORC 80-2, UC Berkeley. https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA084551.pdf. Gives the skill: novices apply context free rules, experts read the situation first.
- Kahneman, D., Klein, G. (2009). Conditions for intuitive expertise: a failure to disagree. American Psychologist 64(6), 515-526. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19739881/. Gives the skill: trust expert intuition only in regular fields with fast feedback; confidence is not evidence.
- Kahneman, D., Klein, G. (2010). Strategic decisions: when can you trust your gut? McKinsey Quarterly. https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/strategic-decisions-when-can-you-trust-your-gut. Gives the skill: quotable plain language version of the conditions and of the pre-mortem.
- Schoenfeld, A. H. (1985). Mathematical Problem Solving. Academic Press. Gives the skill: novices commit early and never monitor, experts plan and abandon failing approaches.
- Getzels, J. W., Csikszentmihalyi, M. (1976). The Creative Vision: A Longitudinal Study of Problem Finding in Art. Wiley. Gives the skill: keeping the problem open longer before committing predicts better work.
- Wedell-Wedellsborg, T. (2017). Are you solving the right problems? Harvard Business Review, January-February 2017. https://hbr.org/2017/01/are-you-solving-the-right-problems. Gives the skill: reframing (the slow elevator) as a diagnosis step before solving.
- Wineburg, S. S. (1991). Historical problem solving: a study of the cognitive processes used in the evaluation of documentary and pictorial evidence. Journal of Educational Psychology 83(1), 73-87. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.1.73. Gives the skill: sourcing, contextualization and corroboration of primary material.
- Wineburg, S., McGrew, S. (2017). Lateral reading: reading less and learning more when evaluating digital information. Stanford History Education Group Working Paper 2017-A1. https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2017/10/fact-checkers-outperform-historians-evaluating-online-information. Gives the skill: judge a source by what independent sources say about it.
- Simkin, M. V., Roychowdhury, V. P. (2003). Read before you cite! Complex Systems 14(3), 269-274. https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0212043. Gives the skill: evidence that working from summaries and citations propagates errors.
- Polya, G. (1945). How to Solve It. Princeton University Press. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_to_Solve_It. Gives the skill: understand, related problem, plan, look back.
- Lafley, A. G., Martin, R. L. (2013). Playing to Win: How Strategy Really Works. Harvard Business Review Press. Gives the skill: the "what would have to be true?" question for testing options.
- Christensen, C. M., Hall, T., Dillon, K., Duncan, D. S. (2016). Know your customers' "jobs to be done". Harvard Business Review, September 2016. https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done. Gives the skill: ask what progress the requester is trying to make, not what feature they named.
- The XY problem. https://xyproblem.info and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XY_problem. Gives the skill: name for asking about the attempted solution instead of the goal.
- Gawande, A. (2009). The Checklist Manifesto: How to Get Things Right. Metropolitan Books. Gives the skill: short lists of killer items at a pause point catch what experts skip.
- Haynes, A. B., et al. (2009). A surgical safety checklist to reduce morbidity and mortality in a global population. New England Journal of Medicine 360(5), 491-499. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19144931/. Gives the skill: hard evidence that a 19 item checklist cut deaths from 1.5% to 0.8%.
- UK Ministry of Defence, DCDC (2021). Red Teaming Handbook, 3rd edition. https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/1027158/20210625-Red_Teaming_Handbook.pdf. Gives the skill: named techniques for challenging a plan from outside it.
- Tetlock, P. E., Gardner, D. (2015). Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction. Crown. https://goodjudgment.com/philip-tetlocks-10-commandments-of-superforecasting/. Gives the skill: Fermi-ize the problem and balance inside and outside views.
- Weinstein, L., Adam, J. A. (2008). Guesstimation: Solving the World's Problems on the Back of a Cocktail Napkin. Princeton University Press. Gives the skill: order of magnitude estimates as a sanity check.
- Lovallo, D., Kahneman, D. (2003). Delusions of success: how optimism undermines executives' decisions. Harvard Business Review 81(7), 56-63. https://hbr.org/2003/07/delusions-of-success-how-optimism-undermines-executives-decisions. Gives the skill: reference class forecasting, the outside view check.
- Kahneman, D. (2011). Thinking, Fast and Slow. Farrar, Straus and Giroux. Gives the skill: inside versus outside view and the planning fallacy in plain language.
- Kahneman, D., Sibony, O., Sunstein, C. R. (2021). Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment. Little, Brown. Gives the skill: collect judgments independently before comparing them (decision hygiene).
- Denzin, N. K. (1970; 2nd ed. 1978). The Research Act. Aldine. Gives the skill: triangulation by data, investigator, theory and method.
- Feynman, R. P. (1974). Cargo cult science. Engineering and Science 37(7), 10-13. https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/51/2/CargoCult.htm. Gives the skill: you are the easiest person to fool, so report what could make the result invalid.
- Huang, J., et al. (2023). Large language models cannot self-correct reasoning yet. ICLR 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798. Gives the skill: an agent reviewing its own reasoning is not a valid check.
- Dhuliawala, S., et al. (2023). Chain-of-verification reduces hallucination in large language models. Findings of ACL 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11495. Gives the skill: answer verification questions independently of the draft.
- Zheng, H. S., Mishra, S., et al. (2023). Take a step back: evoking reasoning via abstraction in large language models. ICLR 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06117. Gives the skill: asking first for the underlying principle improves LLM answers, the AI version of deep structure.
- Zheng, M., Pei, J., Jurgens, D. (2023, rev. 2024). When "A helpful assistant" is not really helpful: personas in system prompts do not improve performances of large language models. https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10054. Gives the skill: generic personas do not help, so the expert step must be specific.
- Kim, J., Yang, N., Jung, K. (2024). Persona is a double-edged sword: mitigating the negative impact of role-playing prompts in zero-shot reasoning tasks. https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08631. Gives the skill: personas can hurt; check the persona's answer against a neutral one.
- Salewski, L., Alaniz, S., Rio-Torto, I., Schulz, E., Akata, Z. (2023). In-context impersonation reveals large language models' strengths and biases. NeurIPS 2023. https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14930. Gives the skill: domain fit of the impersonated expert matters.
- Xu, B., et al. (2023). ExpertPrompting: instructing large language models to be distinguished experts. https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14688. Gives the skill: have the model write a detailed expert description for this exact request.
- Kong, A., et al. (2023). Better zero-shot reasoning with role-play prompting. NAACL 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.07702. Gives the skill: role play can trigger better reasoning when the role fits.
- Anthropic. Prompting best practices (Claude docs). https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/claude-prompting-best-practices. Gives the skill: give a role and the motivation, and avoid overengineering beyond the request.

## Prompting and agents

- Zheng, Pei, Logeswaran, Lee, Jurgens (2024). "When 'A Helpful Assistant' Is Not Really Helpful: Personas in System Prompts Do Not Improve Performances of LLMs". https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10054 . Gives: a persona label alone does not raise accuracy (162 personas, 2,410 questions), so step 1 must produce moves, not a title.
- Basil, Shapiro, Shapiro, Mollick, Mollick, Meincke (2025). "Prompting Science Report 4: Playing Pretend: Expert Personas Don't Improve Factual Accuracy". https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.05858 . Gives: domain matched expert personas give no reliable gain on GPQA and MMLU-Pro, low knowledge personas hurt.
- Gupta et al. (2024). "Bias Runs Deep: Implicit Reasoning Biases in Persona-Assigned LLMs". https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04892 . Gives: personas can cut accuracy sharply and cause refusals, so never use demographic personas.
- Suzgun, Kalai (2024). "Meta-Prompting: Enhancing Language Models with Task-Agnostic Scaffolding". https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.12954 . Gives: decomposition plus tailored instructions plus a tool beat plain expert prompting by 17.3%.
- Zheng, Mishra et al. (2024). "Take a Step Back: Evoking Reasoning via Abstraction in LLMs". https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.06117 . Gives: asking for the governing principle first (+7% to +27%), support for step 2.
- Deng, Zhang, Chen, Gu (2023). "Rephrase and Respond". https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.04205 . Gives: rewriting the question before answering helps, support for step 6.
- Press et al. (2023). "Measuring and Narrowing the Compositionality Gap in Language Models" (self-ask). https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03350 . Gives: explicit sub questions, answered with search, close the gap between knowing parts and answering the whole.
- Zhou et al. (2023). "Least-to-Most Prompting Enables Complex Reasoning in LLMs". https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10625 . Gives: decompose then solve in order.
- Wang et al. (2023). "Plan-and-Solve Prompting". https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04091 . Gives: plan first, then execute, beats plain zero shot reasoning.
- Zhou et al. (2023). "Large Language Models Are Human-Level Prompt Engineers" (APE). https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.01910 . Gives: prompt rewrites must be scored against examples, which the evals folder should do.
- Yang et al. (2024). "Large Language Models as Optimizers" (OPRO). https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03409 . Gives: optimized prompts beat human ones (up to 8% GSM8K, 50% BBH), again only with a scorer.
- Sclar, Choi, Tsvetkov, Suhr (2024). "Quantifying Language Models' Sensitivity to Spurious Features in Prompt Design". https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.11324 . Gives: formatting alone moved accuracy up to 76 points, so test wording claims.
- Huang et al. (2024). "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet". https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798 . Gives: self review without outside feedback fails or hurts (CommonSenseQA 75.8 to 38.1), the reason step 5 must be external.
- Kamoi et al. (2024). "When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes? A Critical Survey". https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.01297 . Gives: self-correction works only with reliable external feedback or special tasks.
- Madaan et al. (2023). "Self-Refine: Iterative Refinement with Self-Feedback". https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.17651 . Gives: self feedback helps open ended quality (about 20%), not correctness.
- Dhuliawala et al. (2023). "Chain-of-Verification Reduces Hallucination in LLMs". https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11495 . Gives: check questions answered independently of the draft (factored) beat joint checking, FActScore 55.9 to 71.4.
- Wang et al. (2023). "Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in LLMs". https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171 . Gives: sampling agreement as a cheap fallback signal when no external check exists.
- Gou et al. (2024). "CRITIC: LLMs Can Self-Correct with Tool-Interactive Critiquing". https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11738 . Gives: critique works when it uses tools (search, interpreter).
- Chen, Lin, Schärli, Zhou (2024). "Teaching Large Language Models to Self-Debug". https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05128 . Gives: execution and test feedback as the check for code.
- Panickssery, Bowman, Feng (2024). "LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations". https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076 . Gives: the model grading its own work is biased toward itself.
- Zheng et al. (2023). "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena". https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685 . Gives: judge biases (position, verbosity, self enhancement).
- Sharma et al. (2024). "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models". https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548 . Gives: the requester's approval is not independent evidence either.
- Sprague et al. (2025). "To CoT or not to CoT? Chain-of-thought helps mainly on math and symbolic reasoning". https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12183 . Gives: prefer tools over prose reasoning, and apply extra reasoning selectively.
- Liu et al. (2025). "Mind Your Step (by Step): Chain-of-Thought can Reduce Performance on Tasks where Thinking Makes Humans Worse". https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.21333 . Gives: extra deliberation can lower accuracy (up to 36.3%).
- Chen et al. (2025). "Do NOT Think That Much for 2+3=? On the Overthinking of o1-Like LLMs". https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.21187 . Gives: 1,953% more tokens on easy problems with the first answer right 92% of the time, the case for a trigger gate.
- Vijayvargiya, Zhou, Yerukola, Sap, Neubig (2026). "Ambig-SWE: Interactive Agents to Overcome Underspecificity in Software Engineering". https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13069 . Gives: asking on underspecified tasks gains up to 74%, models rarely ask unprompted, the best questions are specific and task level.
- Zhang, Choi (2023). "Clarify When Necessary: Resolving Ambiguity Through Interaction with LMs". https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09469 . Gives: targeted clarification doubles the gain of random clarification.
- Zhang et al. (2024). "CLAMBER: A Benchmark of Identifying and Clarifying Ambiguous Information Needs in LLMs". https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12063 . Gives: models detect ambiguity poorly, so the skill needs an explicit ambiguity test.
- Min et al. (2020). "AmbigQA: Answering Ambiguous Open-domain Questions". https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.10645 . Gives: ambiguity in real questions is common.
- Dubois, Galambosi, Liang, Hashimoto (2024). "Length-Controlled AlpacaEval". https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475 . Gives: judges reward length, so evals must score length apart from depth.
- Singhal, Goyal, Xu, Durrett (2023). "A Long Way to Go: Investigating Length Correlations in RLHF". https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03716 . Gives: training pushes models toward longer outputs, so size must be fixed in the rewrite.
- Saito et al. (2023). "Verbosity Bias in Preference Labeling by Large Language Models". https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10076 . Gives: LLM labelers prefer longer answers.
- Walters, Wilder (2023). "Fabrication and errors in the bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT". Scientific Reports. https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41032-5 . Gives: 55% (GPT-3.5) and 18% (GPT-4) of citations fabricated, the reason step 3 must fetch.
- Mallen et al. (2023). "When Not to Trust Language Models: Investigating Effectiveness of Parametric and Non-Parametric Memories". https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.10511 . Gives: memory fails on long tail facts, retrieve selectively.
- Liu, Zhang, Liang (2023). "Evaluating Verifiability in Generative Search Engines". https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09848 . Gives: only 51.5% of cited sentences fully supported, so check each claim against its source.
- Magesh et al. (2024). "Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools". https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20362 . Gives: commercial RAG still hallucinates 17% to 33%.
- Lewis et al. (2020). "Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks". https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401 . Gives: the base case for retrieval over parametric memory.
- Shuster et al. (2021). "Retrieval Augmentation Reduces Hallucination in Conversation". https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07567 . Gives: retrieval cuts hallucinated knowledge.
- Liu et al. (2024). "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts". https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172 . Gives: fetch the specific passage, not everything.
- Anthropic (2026). "Prompting Claude Opus 5". https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-engineering/prompting-claude-opus-5 . Gives: scope expansion fix ("Deliver what was asked, at the scope intended"), over-verification warning, deliverable length calibration.
- Anthropic (2026). "Best practices for Claude Code". https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices . Gives: "give Claude a check to run", ask for evidence, a reviewer always finds something, "be ambitious" as the person's scope call, interview mode.
- Anthropic (2026). "Reduce hallucinations". https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations . Gives: quote first, retract claims without a supporting quote, allow "I don't know".
- Schluntz, Zhang, Anthropic (2024). "Building effective agents". https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents . Gives: simplest solution first, ground truth from the environment each step, evaluator loops need clear criteria.
- OpenAI (2025). "GPT-5 prompting guide". https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt-5/gpt-5_prompting_guide . Gives: eagerness control, tool budgets and early stop criteria, do not ask what you can find, contradictions are costly.
- OpenAI (2025). "GPT-4.1 prompting guide". https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/gpt4-1_prompting_guide . Gives: agentic reminders (about 20%) and explicit planning (+4%) on SWE-bench Verified, literal instruction following.
- OpenAI (2025). "Reasoning best practices". https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/reasoning-best-practices . Gives: keep prompts simple, skip chain of thought instructions, be specific about the end goal.
